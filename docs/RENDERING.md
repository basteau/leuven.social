# Rendering

The aim is a city that opens instantly and costs nothing while you look at it, on a mid-range phone as much as a desktop.

## How the scene is built

- **Cells, and only what's on screen.** The city is cut into 350 m cells (`cells.ts`). Everything in a cell (slab, ground layers and paths, buildings, trees, parasols) is one merged mesh, and its outlines are one `LineSegments`: two draw calls per cell. The viewer culls cells by their real bounding boxes (three's bounding spheres are far looser), so a frame costs what's in view, not what the city holds. Zoomed in, that's a tenth of the city.
- **Build what's seen first.** Cells in view, and those whose shadows can fall into it, are built for the first frame; the rest are built in 8 ms slices while the page is idle (`requestIdleCallback`). The first paint costs what the visible part costs. Even that is done in steps that hand the main thread back in between (finding the party walls, cutting the city into cells, building those in view about 40 ms at a time, drawing), so the page keeps painting and taking input: the longest task while loading is about 130 ms, finding the party walls, where it was 400 in one piece. Meanwhile the shaders compile on the GPU's own threads (`compileAsync`, with `KHR_parallel_shader_compile`), started for a stand-in triangle before any cell is built, so the first frame doesn't wait on them. The shadow map covers more than the view, as room to pan, and once the idle builder has built the last cell that casts into that room, it is drawn once more with them all. That is the only frame the idle builder asks for (unless a cell it builds is on screen), so a load draws two or three frames, not a dozen.
- **Vertex colours instead of textures.** Every face carries its colour from `palette.ts`. One toon material with a 3-step gradient draws every cell; ground and trees carry no facade, so they get no windows. Outlines use a `LineBasicMaterial`, and surfaces a polygon offset so faces sit just behind their lines. Ink goes only where people read a shape: no base lines (the ground covers them), no line along a party wall to a neighbour within 1.5 m of the same height, nothing under 0.75 m and nothing on footprints under 20 m². A vertex is 24 bytes: position floats, the normal and the palette's sRGB colour as bytes (the shader makes the colour linear), and the `facade` float. Once a cell's buffers are on the GPU their arrays are let go, so the city isn't held twice; a lost GPU context builds the cells again.
- **Indexed geometry.** Every mesh and outline is drawn by index. A flat quad is four vertices, a flat polygon one per corner, and outline segments share the points where they meet (a hash on the position finds them). That makes the city 2.6M vertices and 65 MB instead of 4M and 89 MB as plain triangles, with the same triangles, and 20-25% less GPU time a frame. Every cell has under 65k vertices, so indices are 16-bit (32-bit if a cell ever outgrows that). Draw ranges, and the marks cells keep for them, count indices.
- **Flat shading on purpose.** `MeshBuilder` writes one normal per face and flips each triangle to face an outward hint, so winding mistakes can't happen. Triangles share a vertex only where they share a corner and the normal, as within a flat face.
- **Landmarks in the same draw call.** Hero landmarks (`landmark-models.ts`) are a handful of halls, towers and spires, keyed by GRB building id and fitted to the LiDAR in the footprint's own frame (u along its long side, v across). Their footprint is extruded to a low `base` and the parts go on top, so no polygon clipping is needed. Parts that form an outer wall stick out ~0.4 m past the footprint, so the base never z-fights with them. `landmark-models.test.ts` fails if a rebake drops or reshapes one of their footprints. Stepped gables and café parasols ride in the same mesh too, and so do the towers the LiDAR finds on other churches (`addTower`): a square tower with a spire, or an octagonal spire on the ridge, 36 or 72 vertices each, seven in all.
- **Roofs from the survey.** Near-rectangles get gables, their ridge along the long side or, where the LiDAR saw the slopes face that way (a terraced house on a deep plot), across it: the same quad pair over the rectangle with its axes swapped, no vertex more. Every other footprint gets a hipped roof over its straight skeleton (`src/data/skeleton.ts`), which follows wings and courtyards for a handful of triangles per face and flattens off on wide blocks. Footprints are simplified to 25 cm first; the few the skeleton can't resolve (3 in 2556 around Grote Markt) fall back to flat.
- **Street furniture only up close.** About 4,900 lamps, benches, bike racks, bus stops, statues, fountains and pumps from OpenStreetMap, placed by rule so none stands in a wall or on another (`src/data/props.ts`), a few boxes and panels each (`src/scene/props.ts`), drawn 1.3× life size so a lamp post is more than a hairline. A bike is 41 triangles (hexagon wheels with a pale diamond hub on each face inside the dark tyre, tubes, a saddle, a flat handlebar), so it reads from the street; the city's 8,000 bikes are most of the furniture's triangles. Each cell's furniture is one more mesh with the same material, without the `facade` attribute (it has no windows, and the material reads a missing one as 0), shown from zoom 2 (`PROP_ZOOM` in `viewer.ts`): further out a lamp post is a stray pixel and the frame is already the heaviest. Crossing that zoom refits and redraws the shadow map, so their shadows come and go with them. As the first frame can't show it, a cell's furniture is built after the rest (`buildFurniture`): by the idle builder once every cell is built, or on the frame that first needs it, for the cells in view or casting into the shadow map, if the view gets close sooner. Tall ones (the Totem's needle) ride in the cell's main mesh and show at every zoom.
- **Bridges and tanks are walls.** A bridge's deck is one more flat ground layer, paving a shade warmer than the pavement just above the water, so a street runs on across it. Its parapets (low walls along the sides that face the water, under a paler capstone that overhangs them a little) and the brewery's tanks are extruded straight into the cell mesh with outlines, about a thousand triangles in all.
- **Squares are paved.** Squares carry the setts' texture code, the same grain and joints as the old streets round them, at no geometry cost.
- **Trees grow to their surveyed height.** About 9600 of them: OSM's mapped trees plus LiDAR crowns of 10 m and up, spaced by crown size so canopies close with as few trees as possible. They cost 28 triangles each; the shadow pass draws only their 20-face crowns, as a trunk's shadow hides in its crown's.
- **Detail in the shader, not in geometry.** Windows are drawn by `facade-material.ts` from world position, a few lit by the low sun. Walls carry their top height in a `facade` attribute (plus 200 over a shop front, 400 on a church), and the shader seeds each building's own rhythm from it: as many ~3.2 m storeys as fit, its own bay and window width, and on shop fronts a glazed ground floor under an awning. Church walls get pointed lancets instead, 1.5 m wide every 5.5 m, from 3 m to 2 m under the eaves. When a window is narrower than about two device pixels it fades to an average tint instead of aliasing into stripes. Shade keeps a richer version of each surface's own hue (`lighting.ts`). None of this adds triangles.
- **Roads in the same mesh, detail in the same shader.** The GRB road surface is drawn as pavement, with carriageways, cycle tracks and paint laid a few millimetres above it (`ground.ts`, `paint.ts`).
  - Road faces carry a texture code in the `facade` attribute: hard-edged patches of newer asphalt, the grain and joints of setts, paving joints. Each fades out before it's finer than a couple of pixels, so nothing crawls while the map pans; joints are drawn at least a pixel wide.
  - Paint vertices carry the direction across their mark. The vertex shader widens a lane line to a whole pixel on screen, whatever the rotation, and swaps a zebra's bars for their average grey until a bar spans about two pixels.
  - Paint that only shows zoomed in (lane lines, zebra bars, cyclist-crossing blocks) is drawn last in each cell's mesh. A view where a pixel spans more than 1.2 m stops the draw range short of it, so a zoomed-out frame doesn't pay for it.
- **Railways ride along for free.** A track is stored as a centre line and drawn as two flat ribbons in its cell's mesh: a ballast bed, and a sleeper band whose `facade` attribute holds minus the distance along the track, so the facade shader draws the sleepers (and fades them to a tint when they get smaller than a pixel). Both rails and the catenary masts are ink lines at the start of the cell's outlines. Zoomed out they'd merge into a dark hatch, so the viewer skips them with the outlines' draw range: masts show once a screen pixel is under 0.9 m, rails under 0.5 m (from about zoom 0.8 and 1.4 in an 800 px tall window on a standard screen, half that on a 2× one). Bridge parapets and platforms are a few quads more; platforms are cut at cell borders like the ground, so they never stretch a cell's culling box. Railway land is one more flat ground layer, and Leuven's wave roof is four vaulted bays of 16 triangles, each in the cell of its centre. The whole railway costs about 2.6k triangles and no draw call (`rails.ts`).
- **Render on demand.** Nothing renders unless the camera or the view changes, so an idle map uses no GPU.
- **Hard shadows with a soft rim, four lookups.** Shadows use three's PCF map, with its five-tap disc that turns per pixel swapped for four hardware-filtered taps in a fixed square about a texel across (`shadow-filter.ts`): a shadow's edge becomes a ramp of about three texels instead of a stair-step, still with no penumbra, and nothing shimmers as the view moves. Each lookup starts one and a half shadow texels off its surface on the sun's side, whichever way the face is turned (three would push a face turned from the sun into itself, and the map holds back faces), and only 5 cm nearer the sun. The offset is set whenever the square is fitted (`shadowBias` in `shadow-fit.ts`), so it is 19 cm from the street and 38 cm at zoom 4: benches and statues keep their shadows, and walls in grazing light don't break into stripes. Before, a fixed 0.6 m offset and a 1.4 m depth bias lifted every lookup above anything under about 1.4 m.
- **Static shadows, fitted to the view.** The city doesn't move, so the shadow map renders only on load and when the view leaves the square it covers (or zooms in far enough to deserve sharper shadows). The square is fitted to what's on screen, with 30% room to pan, a size stepped in 19% increments and a centre snapped to whole texels, so shadow edges don't swim when it moves (`shadow-fit.ts`). Its texels stay as small as the old whole-map shadows were at zoom 1 and get sharper zoomed in. On a frame that redraws it, the casting cells show a shadow-only mesh on the shadow camera's layer, so the shadow pass draws only them and the view only what's in view. That mesh shares its cell's buffers, index included, and its draw range starts past the flat ground and the tree trunks, which each cell writes first: the ground can't shade anything, and from above a trunk's shadow hides in its crown's. From the street, where it doesn't, the trunks cast too. While the camera flies between the map and the street, the shadow square spans the map view and neither trunks nor furniture cast: their shadows would be thinner than a texel, and landing refits the square round the walker with them.
- **No hidden walls, no idle vertices.** A wall between two touching buildings is drawn only above the lower one (`partyWalls`). The bake drops footprint vertices within 0.25 m (0.5 m on the fringe) of the line through their neighbours; hero footprints keep theirs, as landmark models are fitted to them. Trees are a 4-sided open trunk and a 20-face crown, 28 triangles.
- **One rendering path.** The canvas renders at device resolution, capped at 2×. Only standard-density screens (below 1.5×) get multisampling; dense screens are smooth without it.

## Walking

Walk mode puts a figure on the streets (`character.ts`) and follows it with a perspective chase camera (`chase.ts`, glued in `walk.ts`): the one other camera, used only while walking. Every frame still goes through the viewer's one render path. It must hold 60 fps:

- **Its own chunk.** Everything only walking needs (`walk.ts`, the figure, its steering, the camera boom, the route hint, the walk grid and A*, and the street-name lookup in a small chunk beside it) is a dynamic `import()` in the viewer, downloaded the first time someone starts a walk, or hovers or focuses **Walk** just before. The map's first load never pays for it. Three.js classes only walking uses still ship with the engine, since three.js is one module to the bundler. The fog lives in the viewer, so starting a walk compiles no shader.

- **Plates on the facades, one draw call.** House numbers and street plates are real geometry in the scene (`plates.ts`, `plate-mesh.ts`), so walls hide them and the sun and shadows light them like the rest. They come with their own chunk (4 KB) and `walk.bin` (64 KB), which the viewer fetches once walk mode has loaded.
  - Every plate within 40 m (numbers) or 60 m (street names) of the walker's 16 m square is one mesh with one toon material, built again only when the walker steps into another square: 0.2–0.5 ms on a laptop (3 ms the first time). The busiest square in the city holds 86 numbers and 21 street plates, about 6k triangles; a plate is a face of slices plus thin ink sides, 50–80 triangles.
  - The letters come from one 1024 × 512 atlas the browser draws once (about 2 ms) from the characters the plates use: each is a slice of enamel plate with the rim top and bottom, so a row of them is a plate and no texture is drawn per plate. Mipmaps and anisotropic filtering keep them clean at a slant.
  - Per frame, nothing but the draw: the walk loop hands the viewer the walker's square, and nothing changes until it's a new one. They show only on foot, not on the map or in the flight, and the shader compiles off the main thread before they first show.
- **Walkability is built on demand.** `WalkGrid` (`src/data/walkable.ts`) rasterises the ground at 1 m in 64 m tiles the first time something asks about them: open, minus water and railway, plus roads, squares, paths and bridge decks, minus buildings, tanks and parapets. A click costs one A* search (`route.ts`) inside a window around both ends, a few milliseconds, then string-pulling into a handful of straight legs.
- **The haze is the far clip.** Fog fades the city into the horizon colour from 90 m to 330 m beyond the walker, and the camera's far plane sits just past it, so the cells further out are culled. The fog is always in the scene (far off on the map) so walking never recompiles a shader. A sky dome (one call) fills the sky behind it.
- **Shadows don't follow the view.** From the street the shadow map covers a square around the walker (45 m, plus 30 m for roofs) whichever way the camera looks, at about 12 cm a texel, and is redrawn as soon as it is 1.6× coarser than that (the map tolerates 4×), so the flight in never leaves map-sized texels on the street, so looking around never redraws it; it redraws only after walking out of it, every few dozen metres.
- **What the camera backs into dithers away, for one compare.** The city's one material and its ink lines drop a fragment, in the same 4×4 ordered dither as the figure's fade, the nearer it is to the walking camera: from 2.4 m in, all of it within 1.1 m (`nearFade` in `facade-material.ts`, one uniform that walk mode sets and the map leaves at 0). The street underfoot is spared (faces looking up, below 0.45 m). A facade, trunk, crown or lamp post right in front of the lens opens up instead of filling the screen, and from inside a house, whose walls face away and are culled, nothing of it shows. No geometry, no extra pass, no blending or sorting; on the map the branch is skipped.
- **Two draw calls for the figure.** It's a few boxes on a twelve-bone `SkinnedMesh`. The ink outline is an inverted hull in the same geometry, and the blob shadow is dithered by the shader instead of blended, so figure, outline and shadow are one call. Its x-ray twin, a checkerboard silhouette drawn only where a building hides it, is the second. The route hint is one more while a walk is under way; dots behind the walker drop off by moving its draw range.
- **Animate only while something moves.** The walk loop runs while the walker moves, turns or idles (four seconds after stopping, none with reduced motion), and while the camera flies, turns or eases. Then it stops, and the map is back to rendering on demand. Nothing is allocated per frame.
- **The figure doesn't cast into the static shadow map**, but it receives it, so it darkens in the shade of the houses it passes.

### How walking should feel

Movement and the camera follow the rules third-person games have settled on, so walking reads as smooth on any screen. Constants live next to the code (`walker.ts`, `chase.ts`, `walk.ts`):

- **Nothing eases by a fixed share per frame.** `x += (goal - x) * k` eases faster on a 120 Hz screen than at 60. Every easing is an exponential decay or a critically damped spring with a half-life (`motion.ts`), exact for any frame time ([Holmér, "Lerp smoothing is broken"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LSNQuFEDOyQ); [Holden, "Spring-It-On"](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)).
- **Fixed-step simulation, drawn in between.** The walker steps at 120 Hz whatever the display does, and each frame draws it blended between its last two steps ([Fiedler, "Fix Your Timestep!"](https://gafferongames.com/post/fix_your_timestep/)). The same keys walk the same path at 30, 60 or 144 fps, and a frame never shows a step taken early or late.
- **Speed and heading are springs, not ramps.** Speed and heading carry their own rate of change, so setting off, stopping and turning curve in and out instead of snapping. The largest frame-to-frame jump in turn rate fell from about 20 rad/s to 3 to 7. Turning is quick standing still (0.07 s half-life) and wider at a run (0.14 s), and a sharp turn slows the walk first. At the end of a route the walker brakes and coasts the last few centimetres instead of stopping dead. Let go, it stops within a third of a second (a 60 ms half-life, standing below 0.1 m/s), under a metre at a run.
- **Collide and slide, against walls as drawn.** The body is a circle 0.45 m across the middle, the character controller's "collide and slide" ([Fauerby, "Improved Collision detection and Response"](https://www.peroxide.dk/papers/collision/collision.pdf)): a step that would run into a wall loses the part that goes into it and slides along instead, at the share of its pace that's left (the cosine of the angle), and whatever it still overlaps pushes it back out at up to 2 m/s, never in a jump. Walking straight at a wall stops at once rather than creeping; a second wall met while sliding (a corner) stops it too, so it doesn't jitter between them. Walls are the footprints', tanks' and parapets' outlines, not the 1 m walk cells (`WalkGrid.wallOverlap`, filed by tile like the rest of the grid): on cells, a facade at 8° to the grid wobbled the body by up to a metre from step to step, and the old axis probes stopped dead at the steps. Along any facade the gap now holds within a few centimetres, a convex corner is a point the body rounds, and a lane a metre wide between two houses lets it through, both walls pushing until it's centred. The edge of the water and the tracks is no wall: the feet (0.15 m) keep off it, through a bilinear blend of the closed cells, so the quay is a nearly straight line too. A step costs three overlap checks of a few dozen segment distances: about 4 µs running along Krakenstraat's facades, under 10 µs a frame. The camera no longer looks anything up at all; it used to make fifty to a few hundred grid and tree lookups a frame.
- **The camera aims, then eases.** Dragging, keys and recentring move where the boom aims; the boom follows with a 35 ms half-life, so uneven pointer events don't judder the view and held turn keys ease in. The look point is a spring on the walker (90 ms) aimed 0.2 s ahead of where it goes, so the camera trails a hair setting off and doesn't lag at a run.
- **It comes round behind the walker's back, but not into the player's face.** While the walker moves, the camera swings behind it, closing the gap at 2.2 a second (a 90° turn is behind it within about a second), twice that at a run and 1.6 on a route. The gap opens as the walker's heading springs round, so the swing eases in rather than snapping. It follows fully after a turn to either side, less from 100° off the walker's back and not at all from 145°: walking toward the camera, swinging round would spin the view. Pitch drifts back to the default too. After a look by hand (a drag, a swipe, the turn keys or buttons) it leaves the view alone for a second, then eases back in over 0.6 s. This is the recentring with a wait that Cinemachine's orbital follow offers ([Unity docs](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineOrbitalFollow.html)).
- **Keys steer by the camera as it was when they went down.** Keys and the stick steer relative to the screen, so a camera swinging round would turn a held key's direction with it: holding D would walk a spiral. Steering leaves out however far the follow has turned the camera since the input last changed (`steerYaw` in `chase.ts`). Hold D and the walker walks a straight line while the camera comes round behind it; press W, or swing the stick more than about 30°, and it steers by the camera as it is now. A look by hand turns the steering at once, as before.
- **The camera keeps the distance you chose.** Nothing pulls it in: a spring arm that shortens for every wall between it and the walker zooms in and out on its own in streets as narrow as Leuven's, and that was the most annoying thing about walking. A house or a tree may hide the walker for a moment; its x-ray silhouette shows through anything opaque (it draws wherever something nearer filled the depth buffer, trees and lamp posts included), and what the camera backs into dithers away (above). Only the street keeps it out: the boom never brings it lower than 1 m over the ground, by raising the pitch (a smooth function of length, so no pop). The flight down still climbs over the roofs (`clearPitch`), so it lands with the walker in view.
- **Zoom is smooth, with steps for the buttons.** A mouse notch lengthens or shortens the boom by about a sixth, whatever unit the browser counts in (Firefox counts lines), a trackpad pinch or two fingers as far as they go, and the length eases there with a 90 ms half-life, so notches blend into one glide. The + and − buttons and keys step between stops 2.5, 4.4, 7.5, 13, 23 and 40 m (each about 1.7× the last), from over the shoulder to a high follow view, starting from wherever a pinch left it. Zooming out tilts the view down, up to 0.45 rad more at 40 m, and zooming in brings it a little nearer level, as map and globe apps do; the tilt is squeezed toward straight down so a drag never meets a dead end. Nothing automatic changes the length, so zoom never fights the camera. At the closest stop at a run the look point leads the walker toward the lens, so the figure dithers away as the camera comes within a metre of it and the inside of a head never shows.
- **A run feels faster without moving faster.** The lens widens by 5° and the boom lengthens by 12%, both following the speed spring, so the change comes on and wears off with the stride. There is no camera bob.
- **A brisk pace.** Walking is 4 m/s and running 10.6 m/s, a cartoon figure a little larger than life, with strides lengthened to match so the legs keep an easy cadence. Pitch stays between looking slightly up and straight down.

### What other apps do, and what we took

How third-person games and map apps handle the camera and movement, and the simplest version of each that suits a small city of narrow streets:

- **Occlusion: keep the camera out of things, not in front of everything.** There are two answers. A probe that shortens the arm whenever something blocks the line of sight (Unreal's spring arm with its collision test, [Epic docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/API/Runtime/Engine/GameFramework/USpringArmComponent); Cinemachine's [Deoccluder](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineDeoccluder.html)) keeps the player always visible but zooms on its own. Cinemachine's [Decollider](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineDecollider.html) only keeps the camera out of geometry and the terrain, and makes no attempt to keep the line of sight. Open-world games pair the second with fading what comes close to the lens, usually by dithering rather than blending, which needs no transparency sorting ([a third-person ghosting write-up](https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/2259316-third-person-camera-ghosting-blender-cycles-baking-other-stuff/), [Opsive's object fader](https://opsive.com/support/documentation/ultimate-character-controller/camera/object-fader/)), and with a silhouette of the player behind walls, as Torchlight does. In Leuven's 6 to 8 m streets a probe pulls in at every corner and every look sideways, which is what felt wrong. We took the Decollider's half (only the street floor stops the camera), the near dither and the x-ray silhouette we already had.
- **Zoom: smooth for wheels and fingers, steps for buttons.** MapLibre animates a mouse notch over 200 ms but follows a trackpad at once, and caps one event's scale ([`scroll_zoom.ts`](https://github.com/maplibre/maplibre-gl-js/blob/main/src/ui/handler/scroll_zoom.ts)). We ease both with one short half-life (90 ms), which glides a notch and barely lags a pinch, normalise line and page units, and cap an event at about 2×. Buttons and keys get clear stops. Google Earth tilts toward the horizon as you zoom in and looks down from higher up ("automatically tilt while zooming", [a user's note](https://rpls.com/forums/software-cad-mapping/google-earth-gripe)); our pitch does the same with length, gently.
- **Orbit and follow.** Cinemachine's orbital follow recentres behind the player after a wait ([Unity docs](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineOrbitalFollow.html)); ours already did, and still does, with the steering latch. Nothing about it changed, except that it no longer competes with a pull-in.
- **Collision response: collide and slide.** Character controllers sweep a capsule and slide along what they hit by taking out the part of the move that goes into it ([Fauerby](https://www.peroxide.dk/papers/collision/collision.pdf)); a capsule's round end rounds corners, and a second contact in the same step is a crease where it stops. A grid of 1 m cells is too coarse for the walls themselves (a slanted facade is a staircase), so the body meets the footprints as drawn and the grid only for closed ground.
- **Turning and stopping.** Games turn on the spot at a walk and in an arc at a run, with a heavier turn rate the faster you go, and stop within a few frames when the stick is let go ([Nesky, "50 Game Camera Mistakes"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-50-common-game-camera-mistakes----and-how-to-fix-them) covers the camera side). Our springs already did the turn; stopping got crisper.
- **Holding a turn button or key.** Repeating controls wait, then repeat: Chromium's own repeating button waits 500 ms ([`repeating_button.js`](https://crrev.com/f968f610359dabba8a722e45b10aa28d5bba5a04/ui/webui/resources/js/cr/ui/repeating_button.js)), its views `RepeatController` 250 ms and then every 50 ms ([source](https://mojo.googlesource.com/mojo/+/02b32f11dbffc023b3d5f849fb09e79dab51349d/ui/views/repeat_controller.cc)). Google Earth's compass arrows move the view for as long as they're held ([help](https://support.google.com/earth/answer/148186)). MapLibre's NavigationControl has no turn buttons, and its keys turn 15° per keydown, auto-repeats included, each a fresh 300 ms ease ([`keyboard.ts`](https://github.com/maplibre/maplibre-gl-js/blob/main/src/ui/handler/keyboard.ts)), which pulses when held. Repeated quarter turns would pulse the same way, and a free turn would stop between the views the isometric map is drawn for. So a press steps at once, as a tap always has; held past 300 ms (the step has landed), the map turns on at a steady quarter per half second, through the quarter views without stopping, and let go it brakes onto the next quarter view ahead, never back (`spin.ts`). On foot it turns like a held Q or E. Keys go by keydown and keyup, not auto-repeat.
- **Not taken.** Trees, lamp posts and benches don't block the walker: a game would give the trunk a collider, but 9,600 trees and 4,900 props of rigid, pixel-thin posts would make walking the squares a slalom for little gain.

## Budgets

The size and render budgets are enforced by `bun run verify`; if one is exceeded, the run fails:

| What                                   | Limit  | Checked by          |
| -------------------------------------- | ------ | ------------------- |
| App shell JS (React + HUD), gzipped    | 90 KB  | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| 3D engine JS (three.js chunk), gzipped | 170 KB | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| Walk mode JS (loaded on first walk)    | 14 KB  | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| Facade plates JS (after walk mode)     | 5 KB   | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| Plates data (`walk.bin`), gzipped      | 96 KB  | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| First-load JS (shell + engine)         | 250 KB | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| CSS, gzipped                           | 6 KB   | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| City data                              | 1 MB   | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| Draw calls per frame                   | 100    | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Triangles per frame                    | 600k   | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Vertices, every cell built             | 2.75M  | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Geometry on the GPU, every cell built  | 65 MB  | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Longest main-thread task while loading | 750 ms | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Console errors and warnings            | 0      | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |

Every frame keeps the same cap, shadow redraws included. A redraw draws the casting cells a second time, so when the view and the redraw together would pass the cap, the redraw gets a frame of its own: it draws only the shadow map, the screen keeps showing the last frame, and the view follows on the next one (`FRAME_CALLS`, `FRAME_TRIANGLES` in `viewer.ts`). Smaller redraws, such as those while walking, share their frame with the view as before.

The three.js chunk loads in parallel with the city data, so the HUD paints before the engine arrives.

The viewer publishes the first frame, the latest and the most expensive frame of the visit, and the smoke test checks all three at every step: default view, after the controls, zoomed in, zoomed all the way out, dragged around out there (which redraws the shadow map for the most city at once), walking a few steps, back on the map, and on a phone. Each visible cell is two draw calls (three from zoom 2, with its furniture), and a frame that redraws shadows adds one or two per casting cell; draw calls are cheap here because every cell shares one material and program, so the budget watches triangles first. Typical numbers for the whole ring plus Heverlee: 34 calls and 239k triangles at the default view, 18 and 123k zoomed in, 94 and 544k zoomed out, 70 and 565k for the first frame (shadows included), 14 and 46k walking.

## Growing the city

The research plan (REPORT.md §2) has three detail tiers, and each building already carries one:

- **hero:** hand-fitted landmarks (Stadhuis, Sint-Pieterskerk, Universiteitsbibliotheek, Station Leuven, Arenbergkasteel, the Abdij van Park church) and the most stepped gables. A landmark's footprint is listed in `LANDMARK_FOOTPRINTS` (`area.ts`), so the bake keeps it whole and in the hero tier.
- **core:** procedural Flemish houses with gables.
- **fringe:** plain blocks.

The map already covers the whole R23 ring and Heverlee. Growing it further:

- Zoomed out is the heaviest view. If it nears the budget, add a coarser level of detail for cells far from the centre of the screen, or raise the minimum zoom, before raising a budget.
- Fringe buildings could skip outlines, or the shadow pass, if they start to dominate.

Run the smoke test after each step and watch the numbers.

WebGPU and TSL post-processing (outlines, palette quantisation, dithering) are the next step for the look. Move to `WebGPURenderer` only when an effect needs it, and keep the smoke budgets green.
