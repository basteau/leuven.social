# Rendering

The aim is a city that opens instantly and costs nothing while you look at it, on a mid-range phone as much as a desktop.

## How the scene is built

- **Cells, and only what's on screen.** The city is cut into 350 m cells (`cells.ts`). Everything in a cell (slab, ground layers and paths, buildings, trees, parasols) is one merged mesh, and its outlines are one `LineSegments`: two draw calls per cell. The viewer culls cells by their real bounding boxes (three's bounding spheres are far looser), so a frame costs what's in view, not what the city holds. Zoomed in, that's a tenth of the city.
- **Build what's seen first.** Cells in view, and those whose shadows can fall into it, are built for the first frame; the rest are built in 8 ms slices while the page is idle (`requestIdleCallback`). The first paint costs what the visible part costs. The shadow map covers more than the view, as room to pan, and once the idle builder has built the last cell that casts into that room, it is drawn once more with them all.
- **Vertex colours instead of textures.** Every face carries its colour from `palette.ts`. One toon material with a 3-step gradient draws every cell; ground and trees carry no facade, so they get no windows. Outlines use a `LineBasicMaterial`, and surfaces a polygon offset so faces sit just behind their lines. Ink goes only where people read a shape: no base lines (the ground covers them), no line along a party wall to a neighbour within 1.5 m of the same height, nothing under 0.75 m and nothing on footprints under 20 m². A vertex is 24 bytes: position floats, the normal and the palette's sRGB colour as bytes (the shader makes the colour linear), and the `facade` float. Once a cell's buffers are on the GPU their arrays are let go, so the city isn't held twice; a lost GPU context builds the cells again.
- **Flat shading on purpose.** `MeshBuilder` writes one normal per triangle and flips each triangle to face an outward hint, so winding mistakes can't happen.
- **Landmarks in the same draw call.** Hero landmarks (`landmark-models.ts`) are a handful of halls, towers and spires, keyed by GRB building id and fitted to the LiDAR in the footprint's own frame (u along its long side, v across). Their footprint is extruded to a low `base` and the parts go on top, so no polygon clipping is needed. Parts that form an outer wall stick out ~0.4 m past the footprint, so the base never z-fights with them. `landmark-models.test.ts` fails if a rebake drops or reshapes one of their footprints. Stepped gables and café parasols ride in the same mesh too.
- **Roofs from the survey.** Near-rectangles get gables. Every other footprint gets a hipped roof over its straight skeleton (`src/data/skeleton.ts`), which follows wings and courtyards for a handful of triangles per face and flattens off on wide blocks. Footprints are simplified to 25 cm first; the few the skeleton can't resolve (3 in 2556 around Grote Markt) fall back to flat.
- **Street furniture only up close.** About 4,900 lamps, benches, bike racks, bus stops, statues, fountains and pumps from OpenStreetMap, placed by rule so none stands in a wall or on another (`src/data/props.ts`), a few boxes and panels each (`src/scene/props.ts`), drawn 1.3× life size so a lamp post is more than a hairline. A bike is 41 triangles (hexagon wheels with a pale diamond hub on each face inside the dark tyre, tubes, a saddle, a flat handlebar), so it reads from the street; the city's 8,000 bikes are most of the furniture's triangles. Each cell's furniture is one more mesh with the same material, shown from zoom 2 (`PROP_ZOOM` in `viewer.ts`): further out a lamp post is a stray pixel and the frame is already the heaviest. Crossing that zoom refits and redraws the shadow map, so their shadows come and go with them. Tall ones (the Totem's needle) ride in the cell's main mesh and show at every zoom.
- **Bridges and tanks are walls.** Bridge parapets (low walls along the sides that face the water) and the brewery's tanks are extruded straight into the cell mesh with outlines, a few hundred triangles in all.
- **Trees grow to their surveyed height.** About 9600 of them: OSM's mapped trees plus LiDAR crowns of 10 m and up, spaced by crown size so canopies close with as few trees as possible. They cost 28 triangles each; the shadow pass draws only their 20-face crowns, as a trunk's shadow hides in its crown's.
- **Detail in the shader, not in geometry.** Windows are drawn by `facade-material.ts` from world position, a few lit by the low sun. Walls carry their top height in a `facade` attribute (plus 200 over a shop front), and the shader seeds each building's own rhythm from it: as many ~3.2 m storeys as fit, its own bay and window width, and on shop fronts a glazed ground floor under an awning. When a window is narrower than about two device pixels it fades to an average tint instead of aliasing into stripes. Shade keeps a richer version of each surface's own hue (`lighting.ts`). None of this adds triangles.
- **Roads in the same mesh, detail in the same shader.** The GRB road surface is drawn as pavement, with carriageways, cycle tracks and paint laid a few millimetres above it (`ground.ts`, `paint.ts`).
  - Road faces carry a texture code in the `facade` attribute: hard-edged patches of newer asphalt, the grain and joints of setts, paving joints. Each fades out before it's finer than a couple of pixels, so nothing crawls while the map pans; joints are drawn at least a pixel wide.
  - Paint vertices carry the direction across their mark. The vertex shader widens a lane line to a whole pixel on screen, whatever the rotation, and swaps a zebra's bars for their average grey until a bar spans about two pixels.
  - Paint that only shows zoomed in (lane lines, zebra bars, cyclist-crossing blocks) is drawn last in each cell's mesh. A view where a pixel spans more than 1.2 m stops the draw range short of it, so a zoomed-out frame doesn't pay for it.
- **Railways ride along for free.** A track is stored as a centre line and drawn as two flat ribbons in its cell's mesh: a ballast bed, and a sleeper band whose `facade` attribute holds minus the distance along the track, so the facade shader draws the sleepers (and fades them to a tint when they get smaller than a pixel). Both rails and the catenary masts are ink lines at the start of the cell's outlines. Zoomed out they'd merge into a dark hatch, so the viewer skips them with the outlines' draw range: masts show once a screen pixel is under 0.9 m, rails under 0.5 m (from about zoom 0.8 and 1.4 in an 800 px tall window on a standard screen, half that on a 2× one). Bridge parapets and platforms are a few quads more; platforms are cut at cell borders like the ground, so they never stretch a cell's culling box. Railway land is one more flat ground layer, and Leuven's wave roof is four vaulted bays of 16 triangles, each in the cell of its centre. The whole railway costs about 2.6k triangles and no draw call (`rails.ts`).
- **Render on demand.** Nothing renders unless the camera or the view changes, so an idle map uses no GPU.
- **Hard shadows with a soft rim, four lookups.** Shadows use three's PCF map, with its five-tap disc that turns per pixel swapped for four hardware-filtered taps in a fixed square about a texel across (`shadow-filter.ts`): a shadow's edge becomes a ramp of about three texels instead of a stair-step, still with no penumbra, and nothing shimmers as the view moves. Each lookup starts one and a half shadow texels off its surface on the sun's side, whichever way the face is turned (three would push a face turned from the sun into itself, and the map holds back faces), and only 5 cm nearer the sun. The offset is set whenever the square is fitted (`shadowBias` in `shadow-fit.ts`), so it is 19 cm from the street and 38 cm at zoom 4: benches and statues keep their shadows, and walls in grazing light don't break into stripes. Before, a fixed 0.6 m offset and a 1.4 m depth bias lifted every lookup above anything under about 1.4 m.
- **Static shadows, fitted to the view.** The city doesn't move, so the shadow map renders only on load and when the view leaves the square it covers (or zooms in far enough to deserve sharper shadows). The square is fitted to what's on screen, with 30% room to pan, a size stepped in 19% increments and a centre snapped to whole texels, so shadow edges don't swim when it moves (`shadow-fit.ts`). Its texels stay as small as the old whole-map shadows were at zoom 1 and get sharper zoomed in. On a frame that redraws it, the casting cells show a shadow-only mesh on the shadow camera's layer, so the shadow pass draws only them and the view only what's in view. That mesh shares its cell's buffers and starts past the flat ground and the tree trunks, which each cell writes first: the ground can't shade anything, and from above a trunk's shadow hides in its crown's. From the street, where it doesn't, the trunks cast too. While the camera flies between the map and the street, the shadow square spans the map view and neither trunks nor furniture cast: their shadows would be thinner than a texel, and landing refits the square round the walker with them.
- **No hidden walls, no idle vertices.** A wall between two touching buildings is drawn only above the lower one (`partyWalls`). The bake drops footprint vertices within 0.25 m (0.5 m on the fringe) of the line through their neighbours; hero footprints keep theirs, as landmark models are fitted to them. Trees are a 4-sided open trunk and a 20-face crown, 28 triangles.
- **One rendering path.** The canvas renders at device resolution, capped at 2×. Only standard-density screens (below 1.5×) get multisampling; dense screens are smooth without it.

## Walking

Walk mode puts a figure on the streets (`character.ts`) and follows it with a perspective chase camera (`chase.ts`, glued in `walk.ts`): the one other camera, used only while walking. Every frame still goes through the viewer's one render path. It must hold 60 fps:

- **Its own chunk.** Everything only walking needs (`walk.ts`, the figure, its steering, the camera boom, the route hint, the walk grid and A*, and the street-name lookup in a small chunk beside it) is a dynamic `import()` in the viewer, downloaded the first time someone starts a walk, or hovers or focuses **Walk** just before. The map's first load never pays for it. Three.js classes only walking uses still ship with the engine, since three.js is one module to the bundler. The fog lives in the viewer, so starting a walk compiles no shader.

- **Walkability is built on demand.** `WalkGrid` (`src/data/walkable.ts`) rasterises roads, squares and paths at 1 m, minus building footprints, in 64 m tiles the first time something asks about them. A click costs one A* search (`route.ts`) inside a window around both ends, a few milliseconds, then string-pulling into a handful of straight legs.
- **The haze is the far clip.** Fog fades the city into the horizon colour from 90 m to 330 m beyond the walker, and the camera's far plane sits just past it, so the cells further out are culled. The fog is always in the scene (far off on the map) so walking never recompiles a shader. A sky dome (one call) fills the sky behind it.
- **Shadows don't follow the view.** From the street the shadow map covers a square around the walker (45 m, plus 30 m for roofs) whichever way the camera looks, at about 12 cm a texel, and is redrawn as soon as it is 1.6× coarser than that (the map tolerates 4×), so the flight in never leaves map-sized texels on the street, so looking around never redraws it; it redraws only after walking out of it, every few dozen metres.
- **The camera's collision is a lookup.** The boom and two whiskers either side of it are checked in half-metre steps against the walk grid's building heights and the trees, then the step that hit is halved four times. The trees are filed once, when the walk grid is built, into 16 m squares by the ground their crowns cover, so a check reads the few trees of one square. A trunk is a post up to the middle of its crown and the crown an ellipsoid just inside the drawn one, both with 30 cm to spare; a tree the look point is in (a low crown overhead) doesn't count. About fifty lookups a frame in the open, a few hundred in a tight spot, and nothing built per frame.
- **Two draw calls for the figure.** It's a few boxes on a twelve-bone `SkinnedMesh`. The ink outline is an inverted hull in the same geometry, and the blob shadow is dithered by the shader instead of blended, so figure, outline and shadow are one call. Its x-ray twin, a checkerboard silhouette drawn only where a building hides it, is the second. The route hint is one more while a walk is under way; dots behind the walker drop off by moving its draw range.
- **Animate only while something moves.** The walk loop runs while the walker moves, turns or idles (four seconds after stopping, none with reduced motion), and while the camera flies, turns or eases. Then it stops, and the map is back to rendering on demand. Nothing is allocated per frame.
- **The figure doesn't cast into the static shadow map**, but it receives it, so it darkens in the shade of the houses it passes.

### How walking should feel

Movement and the camera follow the rules third-person games have settled on, so walking reads as smooth on any screen. Constants live next to the code (`walker.ts`, `chase.ts`, `walk.ts`):

- **Nothing eases by a fixed share per frame.** `x += (goal - x) * k` eases faster on a 120 Hz screen than at 60. Every easing is an exponential decay or a critically damped spring with a half-life (`motion.ts`), exact for any frame time ([Holmér, "Lerp smoothing is broken"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LSNQuFEDOyQ); [Holden, "Spring-It-On"](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)).
- **Fixed-step simulation, drawn in between.** The walker steps at 120 Hz whatever the display does, and each frame draws it blended between its last two steps ([Fiedler, "Fix Your Timestep!"](https://gafferongames.com/post/fix_your_timestep/)). The same keys walk the same path at 30, 60 or 144 fps, and a frame never shows a step taken early or late.
- **Speed and heading are springs, not ramps.** Speed and heading carry their own rate of change, so setting off, stopping and turning curve in and out instead of snapping. The largest frame-to-frame jump in turn rate fell from about 20 rad/s to 3 to 7. Turning is quick standing still (0.07 s half-life) and wider at a run (0.14 s), and a sharp turn slows the walk first. At the end of a route the walker brakes and coasts the last few centimetres instead of stopping dead. Walking into a wall at an angle slides along it, slower the steeper the angle.
- **The camera aims, then eases.** Dragging, keys and recentring move where the boom aims; the boom follows with a 35 ms half-life, so uneven pointer events don't judder the view and held turn keys ease in. The look point is a spring on the walker (90 ms) aimed 0.2 s ahead of where it goes, so the camera trails a hair setting off and doesn't lag at a run.
- **It recentres behind the walker, but not into the player's face.** 1.5 s after the last look input, the camera drifts behind the walker while it moves, faster at a run and on a route. It does this fully when walking away from the camera, a little sideways, and not at all when walking toward it, where swinging round would turn the controls with it. Pitch drifts back to the default too. This is the recentring with a wait that Cinemachine's orbital follow offers ([Unity docs](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineOrbitalFollow.html)).
- **Walls pull the camera in quickly, without popping.** Collision gives a continuous free length rather than half-metre steps. Whiskers either side start the pull-in before a corner cuts the view. It eases in with a 45 ms half-life and lets out over 0.3 s; it cuts only when the camera would otherwise sit inside a house or a tree. This is the probe and lag of Unreal's spring arm ([Epic docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/API/Runtime/Engine/GameFramework/USpringArmComponent)), plus the "pull in fast, let out slow" rule from camera talks ([Nesky, "50 Game Camera Mistakes"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-50-common-game-camera-mistakes----and-how-to-fix-them)).
- **In a tight spot it rises rather than closing in.** When the boom, whiskers included, has less than 2 m free, it climbs to the lowest pitch that has 2 m, up to its limit, and looks down over the walker's shoulder, as third-person cameras do in narrow corridors. It rises with an 80 ms half-life and sinks back over 0.4 s, so there is no pop either way. Where no pitch has the room (backed into a wall, under a low crown) it takes the roomiest. As a last resort the figure dithers away as the camera comes within a metre of it, through a 4×4 ordered dither in its shader (still no blending), so the inside of a head never shows.
- **A run feels faster without moving faster.** The lens widens by 5° and the boom lengthens by 12%, both following the speed spring, so the change comes on and wears off with the stride. There is no camera bob.
- **A brisk pace.** Walking is 2.8 m/s and running 7.5 m/s, a cartoon figure a little larger than life, with strides lengthened to match so the legs keep an easy cadence. Pitch stays between looking slightly up and straight down.

## Budgets

The size and render budgets are enforced by `bun run verify`; if one is exceeded, the run fails:

| What                                   | Limit  | Checked by          |
| -------------------------------------- | ------ | ------------------- |
| App shell JS (React + HUD), gzipped    | 90 KB  | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| 3D engine JS (three.js chunk), gzipped | 170 KB | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| Walk mode JS (loaded on first walk)    | 14 KB  | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| First-load JS (shell + engine)         | 250 KB | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| CSS, gzipped                           | 6 KB   | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| City data                              | 1 MB   | `scripts/budget.ts` |
| Draw calls per frame                   | 100    | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Triangles per frame                    | 600k   | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Vertices, every cell built             | 4M     | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Geometry on the GPU, every cell built  | 90 MB  | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Longest main-thread task while loading | 750 ms | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |
| Console errors and warnings            | 0      | `scripts/smoke.ts`  |

Every frame keeps the same cap, shadow redraws included. A redraw draws the casting cells a second time, so when the view and the redraw together would pass the cap, the redraw gets a frame of its own: it draws only the shadow map, the screen keeps showing the last frame, and the view follows on the next one (`FRAME_CALLS`, `FRAME_TRIANGLES` in `viewer.ts`). Smaller redraws, such as those while walking, share their frame with the view as before.

The three.js chunk loads in parallel with the city data, so the HUD paints before the engine arrives.

The viewer publishes the first frame, the latest and the most expensive frame of the visit, and the smoke test checks all three at every step: default view, after the controls, zoomed in, zoomed all the way out, dragged around out there (which redraws the shadow map for the most city at once), walking a few steps, back on the map, and on a phone. Each visible cell is two draw calls (three from zoom 2, with its furniture), and a frame that redraws shadows adds one or two per casting cell; draw calls are cheap here because every cell shares one material and program, so the budget watches triangles first. Typical numbers for the whole ring plus Heverlee: 34 calls and 239k triangles at the default view, 18 and 123k zoomed in, 94 and 544k zoomed out, 70 and 565k for the first frame (shadows included), 14 and 46k walking.

## Growing the city

The research plan (REPORT.md §2) has three detail tiers, and each building already carries one:

- **hero:** hand-fitted landmarks (Stadhuis, Sint-Pieterskerk, Universiteitsbibliotheek, Station Leuven, Arenbergkasteel, the Abdij van Park church) and the most stepped gables. A landmark's footprint is listed in `LANDMARK_FOOTPRINTS` (`area.ts`), so the bake keeps it whole and in the hero tier.
- **core:** procedural Flemish houses with gables.
- **fringe:** plain blocks.

The map already covers the whole R23 ring and Heverlee. Growing it further:

- Zoomed out is the heaviest view. If it nears the budget, add a coarser level of detail for cells far from the centre of the screen, or raise the minimum zoom, before raising a budget.
- Fringe buildings could skip outlines, or the shadow pass, if they start to dominate.

Run the smoke test after each step and watch the numbers.

WebGPU and TSL post-processing (outlines, palette quantisation, dithering) are the next step for the look. Move to `WebGPURenderer` only when an effect needs it, and keep the smoke budgets green.
