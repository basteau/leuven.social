# Rendering

The aim is a city that opens instantly and costs nothing while you look at it, on a mid-range phone as much as a desktop.

## How the scene is built

- **Cells, and only what's on screen.** The city is cut into 350 m cells (`cells.ts`). Everything in a cell (slab, ground layers and paths, buildings, trees, parasols) is one merged mesh, and its outlines are one `LineSegments`: two draw calls per cell. The viewer culls cells by their real bounding boxes (three's bounding spheres are far looser), so a frame costs what's in view, not what the city holds. Zoomed in, that's a tenth of the city.
- **Build what's seen first.** Cells in view, and those whose shadows can fall into it, are built for the first frame; the rest are built in 8 ms slices while the page is idle (`requestIdleCallback`). The first paint costs what the visible part costs. Even that is done in steps that hand the main thread back in between (finding the party walls, cutting the city into cells, building those in view and then those casting shadows into it about 40 ms at a time, drawing), so the page keeps painting and taking input: on a laptop no task while loading passes about 60 ms, where it was 400 in one piece. Party walls need only each footprint's wall rings, so a roof's straight skeleton is raised when its cell is built, not for the whole city up front (that took the party walls from 90 ms to 25). Meanwhile the shaders compile on the GPU's own threads (`compileAsync`, with `KHR_parallel_shader_compile`), started for a stand-in triangle before any cell is built, so the first frame doesn't wait on them. The shadow map covers more than the view, as room to pan, and once the idle builder has built the last cell that casts into that room, it is drawn once more with them all. That is the only frame the idle builder asks for (unless a cell it builds is on screen), so a load draws two or three frames, not a dozen.
- **Vertex colours instead of textures.** Every face carries its colour from `palette.ts`. One toon material with a 3-step gradient draws every cell; ground and trees carry no facade, so they get no windows. Outlines use a `LineBasicMaterial`, and surfaces a polygon offset so faces sit just behind their lines. Ink goes only where people read a shape: no base lines (the ground covers them), no line along a party wall to a neighbour within 1.5 m of the same height, nothing under 0.75 m, nothing up a corner between two walls under 2 m (a saw-tooth facade's jogs, which turned it black zoomed out) and nothing on footprints under 20 m². A vertex is 24 bytes: position floats, the normal and the palette's sRGB colour as bytes (the shader makes the colour linear), and the `facade` float. Once a cell's buffers are on the GPU their arrays are let go, so the city isn't held twice; a lost GPU context builds the cells again. The few meshes nothing rebuilds (the figure, the spawn marker, the route hint) keep theirs to upload again (`MeshBuilder.build({ keep })`).
- **Indexed geometry.** Every mesh and outline is drawn by index. A flat quad is four vertices, a flat polygon one per corner, and outline segments share the points where they meet (a hash on the position finds them). That made the city 2.6M vertices and 65 MB instead of 4M and 89 MB as plain triangles, with the same triangles, and 20-25% less GPU time a frame. Every cell has under 65k vertices, so indices are 16-bit (32-bit if a cell ever outgrows that). Draw ranges, and the marks cells keep for them, count indices.
- **Terrain at the cost of a few cuts.** The ground follows the landform (`src/data/terrain.ts`, about 2 KB of `city.bin`): heights every 60 m read as triangles, each square split on one diagonal. What lies on the ground (the slab's top, the ground layers, paths, paint, track beds, bridge decks and water, platforms and parapets, the rails' and slab's ink) is draped: `MeshBuilder.drape` cuts each polygon along the triangles' edges, writes its pieces as one polygon so a cut point is one vertex, and lifts each corner by the ground, so layers a few millimetres apart stay parallel without z-fighting. Draped faces turned up keep an upward normal, so a slope is lit like level ground and doesn't show its triangles. Everything else stands rigid at one height (`MeshBuilder.lift`): a house on the exact lowest ground under its corners, so a terrace on a slope steps by its true heights, counting its storeys from that ground floored to half a metre (`MeshBuilder.foot`, `footing`), which the facade shader reads back from each normal's spare fourth byte (the `lift` attribute, no extra memory), so neighbours of nearly the same base keep the same rows of windows and the plates and awnings hang from the same foot; tanks on their lowest corner; trees, parasols and platform roofs at their point; props on the lowest ground drawn under their footprint (the terrain plus the layer on it, `drawnGround` in `drawn-ground.ts`: a pavement is drawn 0.15 m over the terrain and a square 0.2 m), and each parked bike on the ground drawn under its own two wheels, sheared onto the plane through them (`slopeThrough` in `prop-shapes.ts`), a rack's rail on the ground under its two legs; a café terrace on the ground drawn under it, and an awning or a sign on the higher of its house's foot and the ground drawn in front of it. Party walls compare exact bases plus eaves, above Grote Markt, and a neighbour within 5 cm of a wall's top hides it all (`PARTY_TOLERANCE`). Exact bases cost 5k more outline vertices than half-metre steps, as neighbours' eave corners no longer coincide, and 0.3k fewer wall vertices. The slab's sides hang from the ground to a flat underside 12 m under its lowest point. The cuts cost about 5% more vertices (2.69M, 64 MB) and a few milliseconds of cell building; each cell's culling box, before it's built, is guessed from its own ground (`groundRange`), so hills elsewhere don't make it look visible.
- **Flat shading on purpose.** `MeshBuilder` writes one normal per face and flips each triangle to face an outward hint, so winding mistakes can't happen. Triangles share a vertex only where they share a corner and the normal, as within a flat face.
- **Landmarks in the same draw call.** Hero landmarks (`landmark-models.ts`) are a handful of halls, towers and spires, keyed by GRB building id and fitted to the LiDAR in the footprint's own frame (u along its long side, v across). Their footprint is extruded to a low `base` and the parts go on top, so no polygon clipping is needed. Parts that form an outer wall stick out ~0.4 m past the footprint, so the base never z-fights with them. `landmark-models.test.ts` fails if a rebake drops or reshapes one of their footprints. Stepped gables and café parasols (a square canopy with an underside and a valance in the café's second colour, standing on the square's setts, 34 triangles) ride in the same mesh too, and so do the towers the LiDAR finds on other churches (`addTower`): a square tower with a spire, or an octagonal spire on the ridge, 36 or 72 vertices each, seven in all.
- **Roofs with chimneys and dormers.** Houses and apartment blocks in the hero and core tiers with a gabled roof get a chimney stack on the ridge 1.1 m from a gable end (one on most, two on some, none on a few; a 5-quad box, 10 triangles), and by another pick a shed dormer on one slope (a front with a pane, two cheeks and a lid, 10 triangles, where the rise is 2.5 m or more) or a roof window lying on it (2 triangles). The fringe keeps bare roofs, so the heaviest, zoomed-out frames pay the least. About 0.19M vertices in all (`roofDetails` in buildings.ts).
- **Roofs from the survey.** Near-rectangles get gables, their ridge along the long side or, where the LiDAR saw the slopes face that way (a terraced house on a deep plot), across it: the same quad pair over the rectangle with its axes swapped, no vertex more. Every other footprint gets a hipped roof over its straight skeleton (`src/data/skeleton.ts`), which follows wings and courtyards for a handful of triangles per face and flattens off on wide blocks. Footprints are simplified to 25 cm first; the few the skeleton can't resolve (3 in 2556 around Grote Markt) fall back to flat.
- **Street furniture only up close.** About 4,800 lamps, benches, bike racks, bus stops and monuments from OpenStreetMap, placed by rule so none stands in a wall or on another (`src/data/props.ts`), a few boxes and panels each (`src/scene/props.ts`), drawn 1.3× life size so a lamp post is more than a hairline. The 110 monuments are drawn at their own size instead, as one of nine archetypes (`src/scene/monuments.ts`) stacked from one primitive, an upright prism that tapers (`prism` in `prop-shapes.ts`, which the furniture's boxes use too): a figure on its plinth is 90 triangles, a group up to 180, the rest a few dozen; 6k triangles and 12k vertices in all, 7k vertices more than the old one-cone statues. The shapes are code and every number is data, so the engine grew 0.7 KB. A bike is 78 triangles (octagonal wheels, a corner on the ground, with three thin pale sectors of spokes on each face inside a ring of dark tyre; a filled rear triangle, two tubes, a turned fork; a chain guard on the right and cranks facing out, each seen from its own side only; pedals, a kickstand, a wedge of a saddle, chevron bars and a lamp on the fork), 84 with a carrier and its red reflector, 88 with a crate or a wicker basket: 83 on average, so it reads up close, and each rack row gets a 12-triangle rail. They were 41 to 51; the extra 0.28M vertices and 6 MB were asked for, and only show from zoom 2. Racks hold the bikes their baked fill says (`rackFill`), 3,632 in all where they drew 8,000 at a flat 75%, which took the furniture from 778k vertices to 487k. The bikes' detail since brought it to 790k, 606k of them the bikes and their rails (167 vertices a bike), and the whole city, every cell built, to 2.72M vertices and 64.9 MB. `buildFurniture` notes each parked bike's index range, place, heading and look (`PARKED`), so a rideable bike can later take a parked one's place by drawing nothing over its range. Each cell's furniture is one more mesh with the same material, without the `facade` attribute (it has no windows, and the material reads a missing one as 0), shown from zoom 2 (`PROP_ZOOM` in `viewer.ts`): further out a lamp post is a stray pixel and the frame is already the heaviest. Only from the street does it cast shadows: on the map a bench's shadow is a texel or two under the shadow lookup's lift, and redrawing the map's shadow square with the city's bikes and lamps took half a million triangles. As the first frame can't show it, a cell's furniture is built after the rest (`buildFurniture` in `furniture.ts`), by a chunk of its own (6.8 KB with the shop fronts' shapes, the drawn ground and the bikes' detail) that the viewer imports once the first frame is drawn: by the idle builder once every cell is built, or on the frame that first needs it, for the cells in view or casting into the shadow map, if the view gets close sooner. Tall ones (the Totem's needle, a column of 12 m or more) are a mesh of their own from the same chunk and show at every zoom, a moment after the first frame. The chunk takes `MeshBuilder`, the terrain and what it needs of the buildings from the viewer (its `kit`, as the plates do): a module the engine, walk mode and the chunk all import would be split by the bundler into a chunk of its own, three.js with it. Only the palette is (scene/props.ts imports it), a 2.4 KB chunk named in `vite.config.ts` that loads with the engine and counts towards its budget; moving the furniture out took 2.2 KB off the first load.
- **Shop fronts up close.** From zoom 2, with the furniture and from the same chunk (`storefronts.ts`), each shop front gets a canvas awning over its door (14 vertices, its sloped top, valance and cheeks, up to 2.5 m each side and never into the next front or round a corner), the trades people look for a sign on a bracket square to the wall (a board 40 vertices; with a cup, a pint, a loaf or an ice through it, or a pharmacy's cross, 60; a frituur's cone of fries 95), a café table with two chairs where OSM maps a terrace (148), and a potted bay tree each side of a café's, bar's or restaurant's door where it has room and no terrace (`doorPlants` in `src/data/awnings.ts`, 50 vertices each, met by the walker as posts). Under each of Oude Markt's parasols stands a table with two to four chairs in the café's colour (wood under cream parasols), and at the tables people sit with a glass in front of them (`addCafeTable`, `addGuest`: a guest is 116 vertices and their glass 40, and a little over half the chairs are taken); the terraces along the pavements get them too. The tables and chairs close a square 1.15 m round each parasol in the walk grid (`CAFE_TABLE`). In all about 110k vertices, shown with the furniture from zoom 2. Awnings are striped in the shader like the walls' canvas, so they are a small mesh of their own per cell with the `facade` attribute (`AWNING` + kind): on the rest of the furniture it would cost 4 bytes a vertex, 2.6 MB. In all 36k vertices and one more draw call per cell up close; zoomed out they cost nothing. A terrace's footprint closes its walk cells like a bench's (`walkSolids`).
- **Bridges and tanks are walls.** A bridge's deck is one more flat ground layer, paving a shade warmer than the pavement just above the water, so a street runs on across it. Its parapets (low walls along the sides that face the water, under a paler capstone that overhangs them a little) and the brewery's tanks are extruded straight into the cell mesh with outlines, about a thousand triangles in all.
- **Squares are paved.** Squares carry the setts' texture code, the same grain and joints as the old streets round them, at no geometry cost.
- **Trees grow to their surveyed height.** About 9,100 of them: OSM's mapped trees plus LiDAR crowns of 10 m and up, spaced by crown size so canopies close with as few trees as possible. A crown spreads with its height, a trunk doesn't: its radius is at most 35 cm (`MAX_TRUNK` in `src/data/trees.ts`), where a 12 m tree used to stand on a trunk 2.6 m across. They cost 28 triangles each; the shadow pass draws only their 20-face crowns, as a trunk's shadow hides in its crown's.
- **Detail in the shader, not in geometry.** Windows are drawn by `facade-material.ts` from world position, a few lit by the low sun. Walls carry their top height in a `facade` attribute (plus 400 on a church, or 1000 + 100 × its kind over a shop front; every range is in `facade-codes.ts`), and the shader seeds each building's own rhythm from it: as many ~3.2 m storeys as fit, its own bay and window width, and on shop fronts a glazed ground floor under the kind's canvas, striped where the trade is (`FRONTS` in `palette.ts`, read as a uniform array of two `vec4`s a kind), with warm panes in cafés and goods in shop windows. Only the walls within 40° of the way the building's main front faces get it (727 of the 8,063 walls of the old `retail` blocks were real fronts), so the back of a block stays a house. The kind branch cost 0.6 KB of first-load JS. Church walls get pointed lancets instead, 1.5 m wide every 5.5 m, from 3 m to 2 m under the eaves. Each has a stone mullion down its middle, a transom every 2.4 m and a paler stone surround. The Stadhuis is civic Gothic (`ORNATE` in `facade-codes.ts`): tiers 3.6 m tall of pointed niches 1.6 m apart, each with a pale statue on its plinth, over a moulded band. Ordinary windows get a frame (white on brick, bottle green, oxblood or charcoal on a pale render), a bluestone sill, and under one in five a box of red, pink, white or yellow flowers; frames fade out before they're thinner than a pixel, flower boxes a little later. Three more hashes of the same seed give each house its style (DESIGN.md, "No two houses alike"): the window shape (plain, a segmental arch, or tall behind a balcony rail), a cornice, a string course, a plinth, shutters, a front door in one bay in four, brick courses every 30 cm once they span two pixels; a shop front gets a door at one end and a mullion. At night (`setNight`, 0 to 1) a share of the windows light up, warm or cool by their hash, and three shop fronts in four, as emissive light the toon shading leaves alone. When a window is narrower than about two device pixels it fades to an average tint instead of aliasing into stripes. Shade keeps a richer version of each surface's own hue (`lighting.ts`). None of this adds triangles.
- **Roads in the same mesh, detail in the same shader.** The GRB road surface is drawn as pavement, with carriageways, cycle tracks and paint laid a few millimetres above it (`ground.ts`, `paint.ts`).
  - Road faces carry a texture code in the `facade` attribute: hard-edged patches of newer asphalt, the grain and joints of setts, paving joints. Each fades out before it's finer than a couple of pixels, so nothing crawls while the map pans; joints are drawn at least a pixel wide.
  - Paint vertices carry the direction across their mark. The vertex shader widens a lane line to a whole pixel on screen, whatever the rotation, and swaps a zebra's bars for their average grey until a bar spans about two pixels.
  - Paint that only shows zoomed in (lane lines, zebra bars, cyclist-crossing blocks) is drawn last in each cell's mesh. A view where a pixel spans more than 1.2 m stops the draw range short of it, so a zoomed-out frame doesn't pay for it.
- **Pitches by what they're made of, their lines as paint.** A pitch's surface (grass, turf, clay, tartan, hard court, sand, gravel) is only its colour; running tracks lie a few millimetres under the other pitches, so a field mapped on an infield, or a stadium's whole oval, shows on top. The 30 fields the data gives (12 soccer pitches, 8 tennis parks, 9 basketball courts, a track) are laid out by `fieldLines` in `paint.ts` from a centre, a direction and a size, and drawn as lane lines are: 10–12 cm marks that the vertex shader widens to a pixel, with the fine paint at the end of the cell's mesh, so they cost a zoomed-out frame nothing. A field stays whole in the cell of its centre, draped like the rest. Arcs take as many chords as keep each within 20 cm of its curve. All of them are 3.0k triangles and 9.1k vertices.
- **Railways ride along for free.** A track is stored as a centre line and drawn as two flat ribbons in its cell's mesh: a ballast bed, and a sleeper band whose `facade` attribute holds the distance along the track (as a negative code past `SLEEPER_CODE`), so the facade shader draws the sleepers (and fades them to a tint when they get smaller than a pixel). Both rails and the catenary masts are ink lines at the start of the cell's outlines. A track cut at a cell border carries how far along the whole track its piece starts (`from`, the `Track` type in `rails.ts`), so the sleepers and the masts, every 60 m, run on across the border. Zoomed out they'd merge into a dark hatch, so the viewer skips them with the outlines' draw range: masts show once a screen pixel is under 0.9 m, rails under 0.5 m (from about zoom 0.8 and 1.4 in an 800 px tall window on a standard screen, half that on a 2× one). Bridge parapets and platforms are a few quads more; platforms are cut at cell borders like the ground, so they never stretch a cell's culling box. Railway land is one more flat ground layer, the lowest, so grass and copses mapped on the embankments show, and Leuven's wave roof is four vaulted bays of 16 triangles, each in the cell of its centre. The whole railway costs about 2.6k triangles and no draw call (`rails.ts`).
- **Map controls of our own.** Panning and zooming the map is `map-controls.ts`: a drag keeps the ground under the finger, two fingers pinch about their middle and drag, the wheel zooms about the cursor (a notch of 100 px by 5%, lines and pages counted as pixels) and the arrow keys pan as a 7 px drag. three's `MapControls` did this with rotation turned off, for 4.4 KB of the first load where these take about 1 KB.
- **Render on demand.** Nothing renders unless the camera or the view changes, so an idle map uses no GPU. The one exception is asked for: with the day passing (P), a frame loop of its own advances the clock and draws every frame, and stops with it.
- **Traffic is three instanced meshes and their ink.** The driveable ways come baked from OSM, split at their junctions (\`src/data/traffic.ts\`, with the graph the viewer joins them into); the traffic chunk (\`traffic.ts\`, loaded once the first frame is drawn, 5 KB) drives about 530 vehicles over the whole map with a trimmed intelligent-driver model (a cruise by kind and road, a gap to the one ahead found by sorting the vehicles by edge each step, a speed cap for the corner chosen next, a random turn at each node) and draws each kind as one \`InstancedMesh\` (a hatchback 68 triangles, a bus 60, a cyclist 56) with an inverted ink hull sharing its matrices: six draw calls and about 30k triangles whatever the zoom, nothing culled, nothing allocated per frame. Vehicles take the toon shading and the static shadow map but cast none; the \`paint\` attribute colours the painted parts per instance and the lamps glow with the night uniform. While traffic runs (or the day passes) the viewer keeps a frame loop of its own and draws every frame; it is the one thing that stops render-on-demand, and C stops it.
- **Day and night cost nothing but a redraw of the shadows.** The light at an hour (\`daylight.ts\`, pure, tested) sets the sun's and the hemisphere light's colours and intensities, the fog, the walking sky's two uniforms, the page's sky tokens through \`onDaylight\`, and one \`uNight\` uniform the facade shader reads: how many windows and shop fronts are lit, as emissive light the toon shading leaves alone. The shadow map's sun moves in quarter-hour steps (\`sunStep\`), each a refit and a redraw of the static shadow map, so a passing day redraws it every two seconds rather than every frame. The lamps' light is a mesh per cell built with the furniture (\`addLampGlow\`: a lit lantern and a 12-sided pool on the street, 22 triangles a lamp), drawn by an unlit material that discards fragments in the ordered dither by how far on the lamps are (\`createGlowMaterial\`): no blending, no sorting, one draw call a cell at night at every zoom, nothing by day.
- **Hard shadows with a soft rim, four lookups.** Shadows use three's PCF map, with its five-tap disc that turns per pixel swapped for four hardware-filtered taps in a fixed square about a texel across (`shadow-filter.ts`): a shadow's edge becomes a ramp of about three texels instead of a stair-step, still with no penumbra, and nothing shimmers as the view moves. Each lookup starts one and a half shadow texels off its surface on the sun's side, whichever way the face is turned (three would push a face turned from the sun into itself, and the map holds back faces), and only 5 cm nearer the sun. The offset is set whenever the square is fitted (`shadowBias` in `shadow-fit.ts`), so it is 19 cm from the street and 38 cm at zoom 4: benches and statues keep their shadows, and walls in grazing light don't break into stripes. Before, a fixed 0.6 m offset and a 1.4 m depth bias lifted every lookup above anything under about 1.4 m.
- **Static shadows, fitted to the view.** The city doesn't move, so the shadow map renders only on load and when the view leaves the square it covers (or zooms in far enough to deserve sharper shadows). The square is fitted to what's on screen, with 30% room to pan, a size stepped in 19% increments and a centre snapped to whole texels, so shadow edges don't swim when it moves (`shadow-fit.ts`). Its texels stay as small as the old whole-map shadows were at zoom 1 and get sharper zoomed in: the map redraws the square once it is 2× coarser than the view needs, with min(60 m, a quarter of the view's height) of room round it, 21 cm a texel at zoom 4. On a frame that redraws it, the casting cells show a shadow-only mesh on the shadow camera's layer, so the shadow pass draws only them and the view only what's in view. That mesh shares its cell's buffers, index included, and its draw range starts past the flat ground and the tree trunks, which each cell writes first: the ground can't shade anything, and from above a trunk's shadow hides in its crown's. From the street, where it doesn't, the trunks cast too. While the camera flies between the map and the street, the shadow square spans the map view and neither trunks nor furniture cast: their shadows would be thinner than a texel, and landing refits the square round the walker with them.
- **No hidden walls, no idle vertices.** A wall between two touching buildings is drawn only above the lower one (`partyWalls`). The bake drops footprint vertices within 0.25 m (0.5 m on the fringe) of the line through their neighbours; hero footprints keep theirs, as landmark models are fitted to them. Trees are a 4-sided open trunk and a 20-face crown, 28 triangles.
- **One rendering path.** The canvas renders at device resolution, capped at 2×. Only standard-density screens (below 1.5×) get multisampling; dense screens are smooth without it.

## Walking

Walk mode puts a figure on the streets (`character.ts`) and follows it with a perspective chase camera (`chase.ts`, glued in `walk.ts`): the one other camera, used only while walking. Every frame still goes through the viewer's one render path. It must hold 60 fps:

- **Its own chunk.** Everything only walking needs (`walk.ts`, the figure, its steering, the camera boom, the route hint, the walk grid and A*, and the street-name lookup in a small chunk beside it) is a dynamic `import()` in the viewer, downloaded the first time someone starts a walk (a tap on the map, or W), or hovers or focuses **Walk** just before. The map's first load never pays for it: all it carries is the tap test (`tap.ts`, the same slop and timing walk mode uses) and three pointer listeners that hand a tap to walk mode, a few hundred bytes. Three.js classes only walking uses still ship with the engine, since three.js is one module to the bundler. The fog lives in the viewer, so starting a walk compiles no shader.

- **Plates on the facades, one draw call.** House numbers, street plates and the shops' name boards are real geometry in the scene (`plates.ts`, `plate-mesh.ts`), so walls hide them and the sun and shadows light them like the rest. They come with their own chunk (5.6 KB) and `walk.bin` (84 KB), which the viewer fetches once walk mode has loaded.
  - Every plate within 40 m (numbers), 60 m (street names) or 50 m (name boards, at most 96) of the walker's 16 m square is one mesh with one toon material, built again only when the walker steps into another square: 0.2–0.5 ms on a laptop (3 ms the first time), and about twice that with the name boards. The busiest square in the city holds about 86 numbers and 33 street plates, about 7k triangles, and on Oude Markt 55 name boards add about 4k more; a plate or board is a face of slices plus thin ink sides, 50–80 triangles.
  - Each plate is settled onto its wall as drawn (`Walls` in `plate-mesh.ts`): the bake hangs it on the surveyed footprint, facing a 256th of a turn, but a hipped roof's walls stand on a simplified ring. The plate takes the drawn wall's facing, stands just off it with its sides 1 cm into it, and hangs at a height from the wall top the facade shader counts storeys under (`plateHeights`, `facadeOf` in `buildings.ts`), between the ground floor's windows and the first floor's, so it never covers one.
  - The letters come from one 1024 × 512 atlas the browser draws once (about 2 ms) from the characters the plates use: each is a slice of enamel plate with the rim top and bottom, so a row of them is a plate and no texture is drawn per plate. Mipmaps and anisotropic filtering keep them clean at a slant. Every kind of plate is a row of one style table (`STYLES` in `plates.ts`), so the shops' name boards join the same atlas and draw call: with them it is 1024 × 1024. A board's cells are clear where a plate's are enamel, and the shader paints the clear part in the board's colour, carried per vertex (its kind's, deepened to 4.5:1 under paper letters, `boardColour` in `plate-mesh.ts`), so the 80 letters the names use are drawn once for every colour of board. A name is fitted to its front (`fitBoard`): the board shortens for a long one, then the name is cut with an ellipsis. On an awning a board hangs in front of its valance, where `storefronts.ts` draws it (`AWNING_REACH`, `plateHeights`); otherwise it is settled onto its drawn wall like a plate, on the fascia over the shop window.
  - Per frame, nothing but the draw: the walk loop hands the viewer the walker's square, and nothing changes until it's a new one. They show only on foot, not on the map or in the flight, and the shader compiles off the main thread before they first show.
- **Walkability is built on demand.** `WalkGrid` (`src/data/walkable.ts`) rasterises the ground at 1 m in 64 m tiles the first time something asks about them: open, minus water and railway, plus roads, squares, paths and bridge decks, minus buildings, tanks and parapets, then trunks and street furniture (`src/scene/solids.ts`, built from the same positions and sizes the scene draws: about 11,600 posts and 2,700 footprints, bucketed by tile once, 20 ms on the first walk). A trunk, bench, rack, statue or café table closes the cells whose centres it covers, at no height, so routes go round it and the camera and clicks pass over it; a post thinner than 25 cm (a lamp) closes none, so a narrow pavement with a lamp on it keeps its route, and the walker steps round the post itself. The bikes left about the city come and go, so they aren't in the grid: a route closes the cells they cover for its own search (`Router.route`'s `avoid` circles, from `loose` in `collide.ts`). A click costs one A* search (`route.ts`) inside a window around both ends, a few milliseconds, then string-pulling into a handful of straight legs.
- **The haze is the far clip.** Fog fades the city into the horizon colour from 90 m to 330 m beyond the walker, and the camera's far plane sits just past it, so the cells further out are culled. The fog is always in the scene (far off on the map) so walking never recompiles a shader. A sky dome (one call) fills the sky behind it.
- **Shadows don't follow the view.** From the street the shadow map covers a square around the walker (45 m, plus 30 m for roofs) whichever way the camera looks, at about 12 cm a texel, and is redrawn as soon as it is 1.6× coarser than that (the map tolerates 2×), so the flight in never leaves map-sized texels on the street, so looking around never redraws it; it redraws only after walking out of it, every few dozen metres.
- **What the camera backs into dithers away, for one compare.** The city's one material and its ink lines drop a fragment, in the same 4×4 ordered dither as the figure's fade, the nearer it is to the walking camera: from 2.4 m in, all of it within 1.1 m (`nearFade` in `facade-material.ts`, one uniform that walk mode sets and the map leaves at 0). The street underfoot is spared (faces looking up, less than 0.45 m over the ground round the camera, a plane walk mode sets with `nearFade`). A facade, trunk, crown or lamp post right in front of the lens opens up instead of filling the screen, and from inside a house, whose walls face away and are culled, nothing of it shows. No geometry, no extra pass, no blending or sorting; on the map the branch is skipped.
- **Two draw calls for the figure.** It's a few blocks on a fourteen-bone `SkinnedMesh`, at most 900 triangles with their outlines (a test holds it there; 877 now, 459 before the clothes, hands and face got their detail, 746 with the old coat, scarf and backpack). Only parts that make the silhouette get an ink hull; the details inside it (collar, placket, buttons, pocket, belt, seams, the face) are flat quads a few millimetres proud, a couple of triangles each. The ink outline is an inverted hull in the same geometry, and the blob shadow is dithered by the shader instead of blended, so figure, outline and shadow are one call. Its x-ray twin, a checkerboard silhouette drawn only where a building hides it, is the second. The route hint is one more while a walk is under way; dots behind the walker drop off by moving its draw range.
- **Animate only while something moves.** The walk loop runs while the walker moves, turns or idles (seven seconds after stopping, none with reduced motion), and while the camera flies, turns or eases. Then it stops, and the map is back to rendering on demand. Nothing is allocated per frame.
- **Feet on the ground as drawn.** The walker, the ridden bike and the bikes left about the city stand on the terrain plus the layer drawn there (a pavement 0.15 m over the terrain, a square 0.2 m), as the parked bikes do: the walk host's `groundAt` is the viewer's `drawnAt`, which reads `drawnGround` from the furniture chunk (the pavement's height until that has loaded). Feet and tyres stand 4 cm over it (`FEET_Y` in `walk.ts`), so their ink never dips into the street, and a kerb's step up or down eases over a 40 ms half-life (`KERB_EASE`) rather than in one frame.
- **The figure doesn't cast into the static shadow map**, but it receives it, so it darkens in the shade of the houses it passes.
- **The spawn costs one draw call, while it plays** (`spawn.ts`, played by `walk.ts`). The marker and its dust are one small unlit mesh (a 20-sided target and seven hexagons of dust, about 200 triangles) built once, scaled each frame to the same size on screen (3.2% of the view's height at the spot, never under 0.55 m) and cut to the target or the dust by its draw range. High up it draws over the city like a map pin; under 30 m of view it is depth-tested, so it lies under the walker's feet. The walker's drop, squash and stretch are its scale and height, and its thrown-up arms and wave are two angles set on the pose after `poseAt`, so nothing is rebuilt. Every part runs on the clock (seconds since the tap or since landing), so it plays the same at 30 or 144 fps, and a hidden page skips to the end. The flight is the old one with a dive (`flight` in `chase.ts`): the pitch gains up to 0.7 rad over the middle of the way, capped short of straight down (1.35 rad), so the camera drops onto the spot from above and then swings in behind, and flown backwards it climbs the same way. A flight turned round midway starts from the lens where it is, so nothing jumps. With reduced motion the view cross-fades: the frame on screen is copied to a 2D canvas over the map in the same task (drawn twice, since a frame that only redraws the shadow map leaves nothing on screen), and fades out in 250 ms.

### How walking should feel

Movement and the camera follow the rules third-person games have settled on, so walking reads as smooth on any screen. Constants live next to the code (`walker.ts`, `chase.ts`, `walk.ts`):

- **Nothing eases by a fixed share per frame.** `x += (goal - x) * k` eases faster on a 120 Hz screen than at 60. Every easing is an exponential decay or a critically damped spring with a half-life (`motion.ts`), exact for any frame time ([Holmér, "Lerp smoothing is broken"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LSNQuFEDOyQ); [Holden, "Spring-It-On"](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)).
- **Fixed-step simulation, drawn in between.** The walker steps at 120 Hz whatever the display does, and each frame draws it blended between its last two steps ([Fiedler, "Fix Your Timestep!"](https://gafferongames.com/post/fix_your_timestep/)). The same keys walk the same path at 30, 60 or 144 fps, and a frame never shows a step taken early or late.
- **Speed and heading are springs, not ramps.** Speed and heading carry their own rate of change, so setting off, stopping and turning curve in and out instead of snapping. The largest frame-to-frame jump in turn rate fell from about 20 rad/s to 3 to 7. Turning is quick standing still (0.07 s half-life) and wider at a run (0.14 s), and a sharp turn slows the walk first. At the end of a route the walker brakes and coasts the last few centimetres instead of stopping dead. Let go, it stops within a third of a second (a 60 ms half-life, standing below 0.1 m/s), in about a metre at a run.
- **Collide and slide, against walls as drawn.** The body is a circle of 0.45 m radius, the character controller's "collide and slide" ([Fauerby, "Improved Collision detection and Response"](https://www.peroxide.dk/papers/collision/collision.pdf)): a step that would run into a wall loses the part that goes into it and slides along instead, at the share of its pace that's left (the cosine of the angle), and whatever it still overlaps pushes it back out at up to 2 m/s, never in a jump. Walking straight at a wall stops at once rather than creeping; a second wall met while sliding (a corner) stops it too, so it doesn't jitter between them. Walls are the footprints', tanks' and parapets' outlines, not the 1 m walk cells (`WalkGrid.wallOverlap`, filed by tile like the rest of the grid): on cells, a facade at 8° to the grid wobbled the body by up to a metre from step to step, and the old axis probes stopped dead at the steps. Along any facade the gap now holds within a few centimetres, a convex corner is a point the body rounds, and a lane a metre wide between two houses lets it through, both walls pushing until it's centred. The edge of the water and the tracks is no wall: the feet (0.15 m) keep off it, through a bilinear blend of the closed cells, so the quay is a nearly straight line too. A step costs three overlap checks of a few dozen segment distances: about 4 µs running along Krakenstraat's facades, under 10 µs a frame. The camera no longer looks anything up at all; it used to make fifty to a few hundred grid and tree lookups a frame.
- **The camera aims, then eases.** Dragging, keys and recentring move where the boom aims; the boom follows with a 35 ms half-life, so uneven pointer events don't judder the view and held turn keys ease in. The look point is a spring on the walker (90 ms) aimed 0.15 s ahead of where it goes (0.8 m at a walk, 2.1 at a run), so the camera trails a hair setting off and doesn't lag at a run.
- **It comes round behind the walker's back, but not into the player's face.** While the walker moves, the camera swings behind it, closing the gap at 2.2 a second (a 90° turn is behind it within about a second), twice that at a run and 1.6 on a route. The gap opens as the walker's heading springs round, so the swing eases in rather than snapping. It follows fully after a turn to either side, less from 100° off the walker's back and not at all from 145°: walking toward the camera, swinging round would spin the view. Pitch drifts back to the default too. After a look by hand (a drag, a swipe, the turn keys or buttons) it leaves the view alone for a second, then eases back in over 0.6 s. This is the recentring with a wait that Cinemachine's orbital follow offers ([Unity docs](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineOrbitalFollow.html)).
- **Keys steer by the camera as it was when they went down.** Keys and the stick steer relative to the screen, so a camera swinging round would turn a held key's direction with it: holding D would walk a spiral. Steering leaves out however far the follow has turned the camera since the input last changed (`steerYaw` in `chase.ts`). Hold D and the walker walks a straight line while the camera comes round behind it; press W, or swing the stick more than about 30°, and it steers by the camera as it is now. A look by hand turns the steering at once, as before.
- **The camera keeps the distance you chose.** Nothing pulls it in: a spring arm that shortens for every wall between it and the walker zooms in and out on its own in streets as narrow as Leuven's, and that was the most annoying thing about walking. A house or a tree may hide the walker for a moment; its x-ray silhouette shows through anything opaque (it draws wherever something nearer filled the depth buffer, trees and lamp posts included), and what the camera backs into dithers away (above). Only the street keeps it out: the boom never brings it lower than 1 m over the ground under it (on a slope, not the walker's), by raising the pitch (a smooth function of length, so no pop). The flight down still climbs over the roofs (`clearPitch`), so it lands with the walker in view; its dive looks down between them anyway.
- **Zoom is smooth, with steps for the buttons.** A mouse notch lengthens or shortens the boom by about a sixth, whatever unit the browser counts in (Firefox counts lines), a trackpad pinch or two fingers as far as they go, and the length eases there with a 90 ms half-life, so notches blend into one glide. The + and − buttons and keys step between stops 2.5, 4.4, 7.5, 13, 23 and 40 m (each about 1.7× the last), from over the shoulder to a high follow view, starting from wherever a pinch left it. Zooming out tilts the view down, up to 0.45 rad more at 40 m, and zooming in brings it a little nearer level, as map and globe apps do; the tilt is squeezed toward straight down so a drag never meets a dead end. Nothing automatic changes the length, so zoom never fights the camera. At the closest stop at a run the look point leads the walker toward the lens, so the figure dithers away as the camera comes within a metre of it and the inside of a head never shows.
- **A run feels faster without moving faster.** The lens widens by 5° and the boom lengthens by 12%, both following the speed spring, so the change comes on and wears off with the stride. There is no camera bob.
- **A brisk pace.** Walking is 5.4 m/s and running 14.3 m/s, a cartoon figure a little larger than life (35% up from 4 and 10.6, which felt slow on Leuven's long streets). Pitch stays between looking slightly up and straight down.
- **Feet stay where they land.** The pose is placed feet first (`poseAt` in `character.ts`): the gait cycle puts each ankle where it should be, and the legs reach it by two-bone IK. A foot that is down slides back under the hips exactly as fast as the walker moves on, so in the world it stays put: flat, then rolling over the ball of the foot as the heel lifts. Its swing is a Hermite curve that leaves and lands at most of that speed, so it neither scuffs off nor skids in. The hips ride as high as both legs reach (a soft minimum), which bobs a walk; a run, down under a third of each cycle, sinks onto each step and springs into half a step with both feet in the air. A stride is what the leg can sweep: 2.6 m walking (249 steps a minute) and 6.4 m running (268). The faster pace mostly lengthens the stride rather than quickening the steps (they were 2.1 and 4.8 m, 229 and 265 a minute), so the legs read as brisk rather than frantic: the walk centres its steps a little ahead of the hips on slightly bent knees, so the longer reach costs the bob nothing, and the run's feet are down a fifth of each cycle, not a quarter, for a longer flight. With the old swinging legs the planted foot slid about 1.2 m a step walking and 2.2 m running; now under a millimetre, which a test holds below 0.1 m against the real walker. Below 1.5 m/s the steps shrink to standing, the walker's acceleration leans the body and moves the hips ahead of the feet or behind them, and turning on the spot lifts the feet in turn, a step for every 45° of heading. The lean follows the walker's `surge`, its acceleration eased over 0.1 s: letting go, the speed spring's acceleration leaps in one step, which leaned the body back 0.15 rad from one frame to the next, and now takes a few. A foot lets go of the hips gradually after it lifts off (half its swing walking, a tenth running) and takes hold again before it lands, and the reach never pulls the hips more than 12 cm below where they ride, so they rise and sink rather than jump as feet come and go: under 4 cm a step at a steady walk or run, where it was 9.
- **More than legs.** The hips dip 0.05 rad on the side of the swinging foot and turn against the shoulders by half the torso's twist (0.2 rad walking, 0.12 running); the legs undo that turn, as they undo the roll, so the gait's IK is unchanged but for each hip joint moving a centimetre. The arms swing 0.55 rad walking and 0.8 running, and the one swinging forward bends at the elbow. Stopping, the body rocks on once and the head nods after it, gone in half a second. Idling (`IDLE_SECONDS`, 7 s, three more than before, for the weight shift) it breathes three times, glances 0.55 rad aside, and drops its hips 0.11 rad onto one leg and back, the other knee bending by IK and the shoulders levelling against it, all faded in and out so the last frame is the rest pose. Turning on the spot, the head leads the turn by up to 0.4 rad.
- **Landing (`settle`).** `poseAt`'s last argument, from 0 as the feet touch the ground to 1 (the default) settled, lands the figure: `landing(settle)` is a quick dip that bottoms out a sixth of the way in and rebounds a little, gone by the end; it lowers the hips 14 cm at its deepest (the knees bend by IK, the feet stay planted), leans the body over them, lifts the head and brings the arms forward. Walk mode runs it when the walker is set down into the street.
- **One rig for walking and riding.** `Pose` swings and bends each arm on its own, moves the hips forward (`hipsZ`) and rolls the body about its forward axis (`roll`) with the legs kept plumb, and `limbIK` (`limb.ts`) solves any two-bone limb in its plane, so the rider puts feet on pedals and hands on bars with the same figure (`rideAt` in `rider-pose.ts`). A rider's thigh can also splay out from the hip (`splayL`/`splayR`, the leg's outermost turn, straight down when walking), so a foot reaches any point: the leg's plane turns about the way ahead to the ankle, then the knee bends in it. That puts a foot down out beside a leaning bike and keeps a planted foot planted while the hips move over the bike. Each arm reaches its grip exactly (`reach`): with the roll, lean, twist and bank undone, the elbow bends to the distance, the arm swings out from the body (`spreadL`/`spreadR`, the resting spread when walking) until the hand is as far across as the grip, then forward or back onto it; the torso stays square, as a twist would turn the hips and the feet off the pedals. So the bars are drawn turned up to 0.5 rad (it was 0.25, when the arms swung only in their own plane) with each hand within 5 mm of its grip, by test; the bike steers as far as it likes underneath.

### Riding

Riding is a chunk of its own (`ride.ts`, with `rider.ts`, `rider-pose.ts`, `bike-rig.ts` and `audio/riding.ts`), fetched when the walker comes within 25 m of a parked bike, or on F. It imports only what walk mode already carries (collide and slide, `limb.ts`, `parked.ts`, the walker's step) and takes three.js classes, the figure's materials and the camera as a kit from `walk.ts`, so no module moves into a new first-load chunk.

- **A kinematic bicycle at 120 Hz** (`stepRider`): the front wheel steers and the rear follows, turning at v·tanδ/L with the tightest radius max(1.5 m, v²/12) (11 m round at a cruise, 25.5 flat out; it was max(1.7, v²/8): 16.5 and 38), the bars reaching full lock in about 0.13 s (a 0.035 s spring; it was 0.21 s) and a heading steered to closing its gap in about 0.25 s (0.35). The bike is drawn leaning 0.45 of the atan(v·ω/g) a real one would, at most 16° (it leaned all of it, up to 39°), on a 0.09 s spring, and the rider's torso sits up against 0.4 of that, so a corner reads as a gentle tilt rather than the street tipping. It never tips over. Pedalling reaches 95% of an 11.5 m/s cruise in 3.1 s and 17.5 m/s flat out (35% up from 8.5 and 13, as walking went up, with the push, braking and easing back raised to keep the same times); braking is 6.5 m/s² (10 m from a cruise), coasting about 0.4. At a cruise the tightest turn is 11 m round, past a street corner, so turning as hard as it can (keys held, or a heading well round) above 8 m/s sheds 3 m/s² until the turn fits: a right-angle corner from a cruise takes 1.3 s, 8.3 m ahead and 7.3 m across, leaving at 8 m/s (it took 1.6 s, 9.3 m and 7 m, leaving at 6.5). Below 1.2 m/s the rider walks it round on the spot (up to 2.2 rad/s) or backs it up at 1.5 m/s, and let go below a walking pace it drags a foot and stops. A step allocates nothing and costs about 0.4 µs, the rider's IK about 0.3 µs more (well under the 20 µs a frame allows them).
- **Collide and slide, a bike's size** (`collide.ts`, shared with the walker): a 0.5 m body circle and a 0.25 m probe at the front wheel. Meeting a wall at a glance costs the speed into it once and turns the heading along it; scraping on costs nothing more. Head on (past 60°) it stops with a small bounce and a thud. It is never stuck: at a crawl it pivots, and S backs it out.
- **The bike is one skinned mesh on seven bones** (frame, steer, both wheels, crank, two pedals) of chunky beams with inverted-hull ink, the small parts unlined: octagonal tyres with a hub and twelve spokes each, mudguards (the front one turning with the bars), a chain and a black guard over it on the right, tan grips, a bell, a lamp on the fork crown (on the carrier in front of a crate) and a red reflector behind, as the parked bikes have; a carrier on stays, a crate or a wicker basket on the bars by its look. 697 triangles, 715 with a crate or a basket, 737 with a carrier, a blob shadow included, at most 800 by test (it was 426 to 450). It shares the figure's materials, so it dithers and shows in x-ray like the figure: two draw calls with its twin. The crank turns with the wheel at 3.2 wheel turns a crank turn (78 rpm at a cruise, 118 flat out: a taller gear than the 2.4 at 8.5 m/s, so the legs keep a city cadence) and freewheels, pedals level, while coasting.
- **Taking a bike from its rack draws the rack copy as nothing**: its index range in the cell's furniture is zeroed (degenerate triangles) and only that range uploaded, with what was there kept to put back; the shadow map redraws once. For this the viewer keeps the index arrays of the furniture meshes that hold bikes, where it lets every other array go after upload.
- **Bikes left about the city** are remembered for the visit in `sessionStorage` and met as circles of 0.55 m radius by walker and rider alike; routes go round them too. The newest keeps its rig, standing on its kickstand at 6° with its bars turned; the rest are baked into one mesh on one bone, one draw call. Past six, the oldest goes back to its rack once neither it nor its rack is in view. On the map none of them draw.
- **The camera riding:** the boom 1.2× longer, the look point 0.35 s ahead with a 150 ms lag, coming round behind at about 3 a second 0.7 s after a look by hand, 8° wider flat out (3° on top of a run's 5). It no longer rolls with the lean (it did, 0.15× of it): the street stays level. It eases from walking's settings to these as the rider gets on and back as they get off, and comes round behind the bike as they take it. With reduced motion there is no lens kick, and the follow is as walking's.
- **Getting on and off are scripted** (`swingAt` in `rider-pose.ts`): keyed tracks for where the figure stands by the bike, how it and the bike lean, its hips and torso, each ankle and the hands' grip, eased through by monotone cubics (Fritsch–Butland slopes), so nothing stops dead at a key or overshoots into the ground. F walks the rider to the bike's left side, the last 0.8 m along it so as to arrive facing its way. A rack bike is rolled back out of its rack until it's clear (at most 2 m, 0.25 s and 0.3 s a metre), wheels turning, and the rider takes the bars as it comes; a bike that must face the open street is turned round with the rider walking round beside it (0.75 s a half turn), stepping forward or back with the walk cycle. Getting on (1.15 s): grip the bars and tip the bike a little toward you, swing the right leg back and up over the carrier, bring it down the far side onto the forward pedal, and step the left foot in under you as you sit, the bike leaning onto it; the walker's pose eases into the script over its first fifth and the script into the riding pose over its last, and the first push on the pedals lifts the foot off the ground. Getting off (1 s, after braking to a stop with the foot down, so never at speed): weight onto the left foot, the right leg up and back over the carrier and down behind it, the kickstand down with a clack at 0.8 and the bike settling onto it, then the walk cycle takes over over 0.3 s while the walker steps 1 m out to the bike's side. A direction held hurries either 2.5×, and once the leg is over getting off it walks off there and then. By test, no joint turns more than 0.2 rad a step at 120 Hz, a planted foot stays within a centimetre, no foot goes below the ground, the swinging ankle clears the saddle and carrier, and the hands hold the grips while gripping. With reduced motion the rack and the turn are skipped and each takes 0.35 s.
- **Walk-grid tiles ahead** are filled while the page is idle, 32 and 64 m ahead of the bike every half second, rather than on the frame a 64 m tile is reached (every 3.7 s at a sprint).
- **Sounds are events:** a freewheel pawl as the wheel passes one (six a turn), the bell, the kickstand, a thud. Nothing per frame.

### What other apps do, and what we took

How third-person games and map apps handle the camera and movement, and the simplest version of each that suits a small city of narrow streets:

- **Occlusion: keep the camera out of things, not in front of everything.** There are two answers. A probe that shortens the arm whenever something blocks the line of sight (Unreal's spring arm with its collision test, [Epic docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/API/Runtime/Engine/GameFramework/USpringArmComponent); Cinemachine's [Deoccluder](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineDeoccluder.html)) keeps the player always visible but zooms on its own. Cinemachine's [Decollider](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineDecollider.html) only keeps the camera out of geometry and the terrain, and makes no attempt to keep the line of sight. Open-world games pair the second with fading what comes close to the lens, usually by dithering rather than blending, which needs no transparency sorting ([a third-person ghosting write-up](https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/2259316-third-person-camera-ghosting-blender-cycles-baking-other-stuff/), [Opsive's object fader](https://opsive.com/support/documentation/ultimate-character-controller/camera/object-fader/)), and with a silhouette of the player behind walls, as Torchlight does. In Leuven's 6 to 8 m streets a probe pulls in at every corner and every look sideways, which is what felt wrong. We took the Decollider's half (only the street floor stops the camera), the near dither and the x-ray silhouette we already had.
- **Zoom: smooth for wheels and fingers, steps for buttons.** MapLibre animates a mouse notch over 200 ms but follows a trackpad at once, and caps one event's scale ([`scroll_zoom.ts`](https://github.com/maplibre/maplibre-gl-js/blob/main/src/ui/handler/scroll_zoom.ts)). We ease both with one short half-life (90 ms), which glides a notch and barely lags a pinch, normalise line and page units, and cap an event at about 2×. Buttons and keys get clear stops. Google Earth tilts toward the horizon as you zoom in and looks down from higher up ("automatically tilt while zooming", [a user's note](https://rpls.com/forums/software-cad-mapping/google-earth-gripe)); our pitch does the same with length, gently.
- **Orbit and follow.** Cinemachine's orbital follow recentres behind the player after a wait ([Unity docs](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineOrbitalFollow.html)); ours already did, and still does, with the steering latch. Nothing about it changed, except that it no longer competes with a pull-in.
- **Collision response: collide and slide.** Character controllers sweep a capsule and slide along what they hit by taking out the part of the move that goes into it ([Fauerby](https://www.peroxide.dk/papers/collision/collision.pdf)); a capsule's round end rounds corners, and a second contact in the same step is a crease where it stops. A grid of 1 m cells is too coarse for the walls themselves (a slanted facade is a staircase), so the body meets the footprints as drawn and the grid only for closed ground.
- **Turning and stopping.** Games turn on the spot at a walk and in an arc at a run, with a heavier turn rate the faster you go, and stop within a few frames when the stick is let go ([Nesky, "50 Game Camera Mistakes"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-50-common-game-camera-mistakes----and-how-to-fix-them) covers the camera side). Our springs already did the turn; stopping got crisper.
- **Holding a turn button or key.** Repeating controls wait, then repeat: Chromium's own repeating button waits 500 ms ([`repeating_button.js`](https://crrev.com/f968f610359dabba8a722e45b10aa28d5bba5a04/ui/webui/resources/js/cr/ui/repeating_button.js)), its views `RepeatController` 250 ms and then every 50 ms ([source](https://mojo.googlesource.com/mojo/+/02b32f11dbffc023b3d5f849fb09e79dab51349d/ui/views/repeat_controller.cc)). Google Earth's compass arrows move the view for as long as they're held ([help](https://support.google.com/earth/answer/148186)). MapLibre's NavigationControl has no turn buttons, and its keys turn 15° per keydown, auto-repeats included, each a fresh 300 ms ease ([`keyboard.ts`](https://github.com/maplibre/maplibre-gl-js/blob/main/src/ui/handler/keyboard.ts)), which pulses when held. Repeated quarter turns would pulse the same way, and a free turn would stop between the views the isometric map is drawn for. So a press steps at once, as a tap always has; held past 300 ms (the step has landed), the map turns on at a steady quarter per half second, through the quarter views without stopping, and let go it brakes onto the next quarter view ahead, never back (`spin.ts`). On foot it turns like a held Q or E. Keys go by keydown and keyup, not auto-repeat.
- **Spawning: GTA's switch, without the cuts.** GTA V's character switch pulls the camera up out of the player in a few cuts to high over the city, holds there, then drops back down in cuts to the next character and pulls in behind them; it takes more or fewer steps the further apart the two are, and GTA Online's sky camera drops into a session the same way ([GTA V's switch modded into GTA IV](https://www.techspot.com/community/topics/modders-bring-gta-vs-character-switching-mechanics-to-gta-iv.194814/); [FiveM's notes on `START_PLAYER_SWITCH` and the sky camera](https://forum.cfx.re/t/flying-cam-gta-online-camera-in-clouds/51248); [the sky camera on joining a session](https://sportskeeda.com/gta/gta-online-player-spawns-air-rejoining-session)). RDR2 mostly fades instead. Our map already is the view from above, so we keep only the drop: a beat while the marker lands, then one continuous dive that tips the view straight down onto the spot before it swings in behind, as GTA's last cut does, and the walker dropping into the shot rather than being there already. Cuts would jump in a city this small; one eased move keeps where you are.
- **Small colliders, met as drawn.** A game gives a trunk a collider; so do we, for the 9,100 trunks (capped at a 35 cm radius, so they aren't a slalom) and the 4,800 props: each is a circle or a box in the same collide-and-slide as the walls, about 0.25 µs more a step. On a route the walker follows the grid's cells, which close where a solid covers their centre.

Every frame keeps the same cap, shadow redraws included. A redraw draws the casting cells a second time, so when the view and the redraw together would pass the cap, the redraw gets a frame of its own: it draws only the shadow map, the screen keeps showing the last frame, and the view follows on the next one (`FRAME_CALLS`, `FRAME_TRIANGLES` in `viewer.ts`). Smaller redraws, such as those while walking, share their frame with the view as before.

The loading screen is in the HTML (its tokens, styles and the little town as inline CSS and SVG, about 6.7 KB gzipped), so it paints with the first bytes, before any script or stylesheet. The three.js chunk loads in parallel with the city data, while the screen counts the city's bytes. The menu is a chunk of its own (about 6 KB), fetched once the city has arrived and the network is free, while the first frame is built: the first load doesn't grow, and the menu is ready when the map is.

The viewer publishes the first frame, the latest and the most expensive frame of the visit, and the smoke test checks all three at every step: default view, after the controls, zoomed in, zoomed all the way out, dragged around out there (which redraws the shadow map for the most city at once), walking a few steps, back on the map, riding and after it, and on a phone. Each visible cell is two draw calls (three or four from zoom 2, with its furniture and its awnings), and a frame that redraws shadows adds one or two per casting cell; draw calls are cheap here because every cell shares one material and program, so the budget watches triangles first. Typical numbers for the whole ring plus Heverlee: 37 calls and 272k triangles at the default view, 25 and 182k zoomed in, 96 and 582k zoomed out, 73 and 593k for the first frame (shadows included), about 37 and 213k walking.

## Growing the city

The research plan (REPORT.md §2) has three detail tiers, and each building already carries one:

- **hero:** hand-fitted landmarks (Stadhuis, Sint-Pieterskerk, Universiteitsbibliotheek, Station Leuven, Arenbergkasteel, the Abdij van Park church) and the most stepped gables. A landmark's footprint is listed in `LANDMARK_FOOTPRINTS` (`area.ts`), so the bake keeps it whole and in the hero tier.
- **core:** procedural Flemish houses with gables.
- **fringe:** plain blocks.

The map already covers the whole R23 ring and Heverlee. Growing it further:

- Zoomed out is the heaviest view. If it nears the budget, add a coarser level of detail for cells far from the centre of the screen, or raise the minimum zoom, before raising a budget.
- Fringe buildings could skip outlines, or the shadow pass, if they start to dominate.

Run the smoke test after each step and watch the numbers.

WebGPU and TSL post-processing (outlines, palette quantisation, dithering) are the next step for the look. Move to `WebGPURenderer` only when an effect needs it, and keep the smoke budgets green.
