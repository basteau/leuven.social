# Rendering

The aim is a city that opens instantly and costs nothing while you look at it, on a mid-range phone as much as a desktop.

## How the scene is built

- **Cells, and only what's on screen.** The city is cut into 350 m cells (`cells.ts`). Everything in a cell (slab, ground layers and paths, buildings, trees, parasols) is one merged mesh, and its outlines are one `LineSegments`: two draw calls per cell. The viewer culls cells by their real bounding boxes (three's bounding spheres are far looser), so a frame costs what's in view, not what the city holds. Zoomed in, that's a tenth of the city.
- **Build what's seen first.** Cells in view, and those whose shadows can fall into it, are built for the first frame; the rest are built in 8 ms slices while the page is idle (`requestIdleCallback`). The first paint costs what the visible part costs. Even that is done in steps that hand the main thread back in between (finding the party walls, cutting the city into cells, building those in view and then those casting shadows into it about 40 ms at a time, drawing), so the page keeps painting and taking input: on a laptop no task while loading passes about 60 ms, where it was 400 in one piece. Party walls need only each footprint's wall rings, so a roof's straight skeleton is raised when its cell is built, not for the whole city up front (that took the party walls from 90 ms to 25). Meanwhile the shaders compile on the GPU's own threads (`compileAsync`, with `KHR_parallel_shader_compile`), started for a stand-in triangle before any cell is built, so the first frame doesn't wait on them. The shadow map covers more than the view, as room to pan, and once the idle builder has built the last cell that casts into that room, it is drawn once more with them all. That is the only frame the idle builder asks for (unless a cell it builds is on screen), so a load draws two or three frames, not a dozen.
- **Vertex colours instead of textures.** Every face carries its colour from `palette.ts`. One toon material with a 3-step gradient draws every cell; ground and trees carry no facade, so they get no windows. Outlines use a `LineBasicMaterial`, and surfaces a polygon offset so faces sit just behind their lines. Ink goes only where people read a shape: no base lines (the ground covers them), no line along a party wall to a neighbour within 1.5 m of the same height, nothing under 0.75 m, nothing up a corner between two walls under 2 m (a saw-tooth facade's jogs, which turned it black zoomed out) and nothing on footprints under 20 m². A vertex is 24 bytes: position floats, the normal and the palette's sRGB colour as bytes (the shader makes the colour linear), and the `facade` float. Once a cell's buffers are on the GPU their arrays are let go, so the city isn't held twice; a lost GPU context builds the cells again. The few meshes nothing rebuilds (the figure, the refusal marker, the route hint) keep theirs to upload again (`MeshBuilder.build({ keep })`).
- **Indexed geometry.** Every mesh and outline is drawn by index. A flat quad is four vertices, a flat polygon one per corner, and outline segments share the points where they meet (a hash on the position finds them). That made the city 2.6M vertices and 65 MB instead of 4M and 89 MB as plain triangles, with the same triangles, and 20-25% less GPU time a frame. Every cell has under 65k vertices, so indices are 16-bit (32-bit if a cell ever outgrows that). Draw ranges, and the marks cells keep for them, count indices.
- **Terrain at the cost of a few cuts.** The ground follows the landform (`src/data/terrain.ts`, about 2 KB of `city.bin`): heights every 60 m read as triangles, each square split on one diagonal. What lies on the ground (the slab's top, the ground layers, paths, paint, track beds, bridge decks and water, platforms and parapets, the rails' and slab's ink) is draped: `MeshBuilder.drape` cuts each polygon along the triangles' edges, writes its pieces as one polygon so a cut point is one vertex, and lifts each corner by the ground, so layers a few millimetres apart stay parallel without z-fighting. Draped faces turned up keep an upward normal, so a slope is lit like level ground and doesn't show its triangles. Everything else stands rigid at one height (`MeshBuilder.lift`): a house on the exact lowest ground under its corners, so a terrace on a slope steps by its true heights, counting its storeys from that ground floored to half a metre (`MeshBuilder.foot`, `footing`), which the facade shader reads back from each normal's spare fourth byte (the `lift` attribute, no extra memory), so neighbours of nearly the same base keep the same rows of windows and the awnings hang from the same foot; tanks on their lowest corner; trees, parasols and platform roofs at their point; props on the lowest ground drawn under their footprint (the terrain plus the layer on it, `drawnGround` in `drawn-ground.ts`: a pavement is drawn 0.15 m over the terrain and a square 0.2 m), and each parked bike on the ground drawn under its own two wheels, sheared onto the plane through them (`slopeThrough` in `prop-shapes.ts`), a rack's rail on the ground under its two legs; a café terrace on the ground drawn under it, and an awning or a sign on the higher of its house's foot and the ground drawn in front of it. Party walls compare exact bases plus eaves, above Grote Markt, and a neighbour within 5 cm of a wall's top hides it all (`PARTY_TOLERANCE`). Exact bases cost 5k more outline vertices than half-metre steps, as neighbours' eave corners no longer coincide, and 0.3k fewer wall vertices. The slab's sides hang from the ground to a flat underside 12 m under its lowest point. The cuts cost about 5% more vertices (2.69M, 64 MB) and a few milliseconds of cell building; each cell's culling box, before it's built, is guessed from its own ground (`groundRange`), so hills elsewhere don't make it look visible.
- **Flat shading on purpose.** `MeshBuilder` writes one normal per face and flips each triangle to face an outward hint, so winding mistakes can't happen. Triangles share a vertex only where they share a corner and the normal, as within a flat face.
- **Landmarks in the same draw call.** Hero landmarks (`landmark-models.ts`) are a handful of halls, towers and spires, keyed by GRB building id and fitted to the LiDAR in the footprint's own frame (u along its long side, v across). Their footprint is extruded to a low `base` and the parts go on top, so no polygon clipping is needed. Parts that form an outer wall stick out ~0.4 m past the footprint, so the base never z-fights with them. `landmark-models.test.ts` fails if a rebake drops or reshapes one of their footprints. Stepped gables and café parasols (a square canopy with an underside and a valance in the café's second colour, standing on the square's setts, 34 triangles) ride in the same mesh too, and so do the towers the LiDAR finds on other churches (`addTower`): a square tower with a spire, or an octagonal spire on the ridge, 36 or 72 vertices each, seven in all.
- **Roofs with chimneys and dormers.** Houses and apartment blocks in the hero and core tiers with a gabled roof get a chimney stack on the ridge 1.1 m from a gable end (one on most, two on some, none on a few; a 5-quad box, 10 triangles), and by another pick a shed dormer on one slope (a front with a pane, two cheeks and a lid, 10 triangles, where the rise is 2.5 m or more) or a roof window lying on it (2 triangles). The fringe keeps bare roofs, so the heaviest, zoomed-out frames pay the least. About 0.19M vertices in all (`roofDetails` in buildings.ts).
- **Roofs from the survey.** Near-rectangles get gables, their ridge along the long side or, where the LiDAR saw the slopes face that way (a terraced house on a deep plot), across it: the same quad pair over the rectangle with its axes swapped, no vertex more. Every other footprint gets a hipped roof over its straight skeleton (`src/data/skeleton.ts`), which follows wings and courtyards for a handful of triangles per face and flattens off on wide blocks. Footprints are simplified to 25 cm first; the few the skeleton can't resolve (3 in 2556 around Grote Markt) fall back to flat.
- **Street furniture only up close.** About 4,800 lamps, benches, bike racks, bus stops and monuments from OpenStreetMap, placed by rule so none stands in a wall or on another (`src/data/props.ts`), a few boxes and panels each (`src/scene/props.ts`), drawn 1.3× life size so a lamp post is more than a hairline. The 110 monuments are drawn at their own size instead, as one of nine archetypes (`src/scene/monuments.ts`) stacked from one primitive, an upright prism that tapers (`prism` in `prop-shapes.ts`, which the furniture's boxes use too): a figure on its plinth is 90 triangles, a group up to 180, the rest a few dozen; 6k triangles and 12k vertices in all, 7k vertices more than the old one-cone statues. The shapes are code and every number is data, so the engine grew 0.7 KB. A bike is 78 triangles (octagonal wheels, a corner on the ground, with three thin pale sectors of spokes on each face inside a ring of dark tyre; a filled rear triangle, two tubes, a turned fork, their two faces 4 cm apart, out past the spokes, as in the wheels' plane they z-fought with the tyres; a chain guard on the right and cranks facing out, each seen from its own side only; pedals, a kickstand, a wedge of a saddle, chevron bars and a lamp on the fork), 84 with a carrier and its red reflector, 88 with a crate or a wicker basket: 83 on average, so it reads up close, and each rack row gets a 12-triangle rail. They were 41 to 51; the extra 0.28M vertices and 6 MB were asked for, and only show from zoom 2. Racks hold the bikes their baked fill says (`rackFill`), 3,632 in all where they drew 8,000 at a flat 75%, which took the furniture from 778k vertices to 487k. The bikes' detail since brought it to 790k, 606k of them the bikes and their rails (167 vertices a bike), and the whole city, every cell built, to 2.72M vertices and 64.9 MB. `buildFurniture` notes each parked bike's index range, place, heading and look (`PARKED`), so a rideable bike can later take a parked one's place by drawing nothing over its range. Each cell's furniture is one more mesh with the same material, without the `facade` attribute (it has no windows, and the material reads a missing one as 0), shown from zoom 2 (`PROP_ZOOM` in `viewer.ts`): further out a lamp post is a stray pixel and the frame is already the heaviest. Only from the street does it cast shadows: on the map a bench's shadow is a texel or two under the shadow lookup's lift, and redrawing the map's shadow square with the city's bikes and lamps took half a million triangles. As the first frame can't show it, a cell's furniture is built after the rest (`buildFurniture` in `furniture.ts`), by a chunk of its own (6.8 KB with the shop fronts' shapes, the drawn ground and the bikes' detail) that the viewer imports once the first frame is drawn: by the idle builder once every cell is built, or on the frame that first needs it, for the cells in view or casting into the shadow map, if the view gets close sooner. Tall ones (the Totem's needle, a column of 12 m or more) are a mesh of their own from the same chunk and show at every zoom, a moment after the first frame. The chunk takes `MeshBuilder`, the terrain and what it needs of the buildings from the viewer (its `kit`): a module the engine, walk mode and the chunk all import would be split by the bundler into a chunk of its own, three.js with it. Only the palette is (scene/props.ts imports it), a 2.4 KB chunk named in `vite.config.ts` that loads with the engine and counts towards its budget; moving the furniture out took 2.2 KB off the first load.
- **Shop fronts up close.** From zoom 2, with the furniture and from the same chunk (`storefronts.ts`), each shop front gets a canvas awning over its door (14 vertices, its sloped top, valance and cheeks, up to 2.5 m each side and never into the next front or round a corner), every trade its symbol on a bracket square to the wall, drawn 1.4× life size like the furniture (`signs.ts`: a cup, a pint, a croissant, an ice cream, a cross, a coin, an open book, a shirt, a bike, a basket of fruit, a knife and fork, a burger, a shopping bag, a cone of fries; 60 to 470 vertices, about 110 on average, on a board in the trade's colour where the symbol alone would be lost against a wall), a barber's pole by a hairdresser's door (220), a beer barrel by a bar's (136), a greengrocer's crates (136 a side) and a florist's buckets (234 a side) where a café's bay trees would stand, the public places' signs (a university's banner 60, a hotel's three flags 120, a museum's hangings 80, a cinema's or theatre's marquee 240, a pencil, a book, a red cross or a ball on a bracket), a café table with two chairs where OSM maps a terrace (148), and a potted bay tree each side of a café's, bar's or restaurant's door where it has room and no terrace (`doorPlants` in `src/data/awnings.ts`, 50 vertices each, met by the walker as posts). Under each of Oude Markt's parasols stands a table with two to four chairs in the café's colour (wood under cream parasols), and at the tables people sit with a glass in front of them (`addCafeTable`, `addGuest`: a guest is 116 vertices and their glass 40, and a little over half the chairs are taken); the terraces along the pavements get them too. The tables and chairs close a square 1.15 m round each parasol in the walk grid (`CAFE_TABLE`). In all about 220k vertices, the signs and what stands by the doors 108k of them, shown with the furniture from zoom 2. Awnings are striped in the shader like the walls' canvas, so they are a small mesh of their own per cell with the `facade` attribute (`AWNING` + kind): on the rest of the furniture it would cost 4 bytes a vertex, 2.6 MB. In all 36k vertices and one more draw call per cell up close; zoomed out they cost nothing. A terrace's footprint closes its walk cells like a bench's (`walkSolids`).
- **Bridges and tanks are walls.** A bridge's deck is one more flat ground layer, paving a shade warmer than the pavement just above the water, so a street runs on across it. Its parapets (low walls along the sides that face the water, under a paler capstone that overhangs them a little) and the brewery's tanks are extruded straight into the cell mesh with outlines, about a thousand triangles in all.
- **Squares are paved.** Squares carry the setts' texture code, the same grain and joints as the old streets round them, at no geometry cost.
- **Trees grow to their surveyed height.** About 9,100 of them: OSM's mapped trees plus LiDAR crowns of 10 m and up, spaced by crown size so canopies close with as few trees as possible. A crown spreads with its height, a trunk doesn't: its radius is at most 35 cm (`MAX_TRUNK` in `src/data/trees.ts`), where a 12 m tree used to stand on a trunk 2.6 m across. They cost 28 triangles each; the shadow pass draws only their 20-face crowns, as a trunk's shadow hides in its crown's.
- **Detail in the shader, not in geometry.** Windows are drawn by `facade-material.ts` from world position, a few lit by the low sun. Walls carry their top height in a `facade` attribute (plus 400 on a church, or 1000 + 100 × its kind over a shop front; every range is in `facade-codes.ts`), and the shader seeds each building's own rhythm from it: as many ~3.2 m storeys as fit, its own bay and window width, and on shop fronts a glazed ground floor under the kind's canvas, striped where the trade is (`FRONTS` in `palette.ts`, read as a uniform array of two `vec4`s a kind), with warm panes in cafés and goods in shop windows. Only the walls within 40° of the way the building's main front faces get it (727 of the 8,063 walls of the old `retail` blocks were real fronts), so the back of a block stays a house. The kind branch cost 0.6 KB of first-load JS. Church walls get pointed lancets instead, 1.5 m wide every 5.5 m, from 3 m to 2 m under the eaves. Each has a stone mullion down its middle, a transom every 2.4 m and a paler stone surround. The Stadhuis is civic Gothic (`ORNATE` in `facade-codes.ts`): tiers 3.6 m tall of pointed niches 1.6 m apart, each with a pale statue on its plinth, over a moulded band. Ordinary windows get a frame (white on brick, bottle green, oxblood or charcoal on a pale render), a bluestone sill, and under one in five a box of red, pink, white or yellow flowers; frames fade out before they're thinner than a pixel, flower boxes a little later. Three more hashes of the same seed give each house its style (DESIGN.md, "No two houses alike"): the window shape (plain, a segmental arch, or tall behind a balcony rail), a cornice, a string course, a plinth, shutters, a front door in one bay in four, brick courses every 30 cm once they span two pixels; a shop front gets a door at one end and a mullion. At night (`setNight`, 0 to 1) a share of the windows light up, warm or cool by their hash, and three shop fronts in four, as emissive light the toon shading leaves alone. When a window is narrower than about two device pixels it fades to an average tint instead of aliasing into stripes. Shade keeps a richer version of each surface's own hue (`lighting.ts`). None of this adds triangles.
- **Roads in the same mesh, detail in the same shader.** The GRB road surface is drawn as pavement, with carriageways, cycle tracks and paint laid a few millimetres above it (`ground.ts`, `paint.ts`).
  - Road faces carry a texture code in the `facade` attribute: hard-edged patches of newer asphalt, the grain and joints of setts, paving joints. Each fades out before it's finer than a couple of pixels, so nothing crawls while the map pans; joints are drawn at least a pixel wide.
  - Paint vertices carry the direction across their mark. The vertex shader widens a lane line to a whole pixel on screen, whatever the rotation, and swaps a zebra's bars for their average grey until a bar spans about two pixels.
  - Paint that only shows zoomed in (lane lines, zebra bars, cyclist-crossing blocks) is drawn last in each cell's mesh. A view where a pixel spans more than 1.2 m stops the draw range short of it, so a zoomed-out frame doesn't pay for it.
- **Pitches by what they're made of, their lines as paint.** A pitch's surface (grass, turf, clay, tartan, hard court, sand, gravel) is only its colour; running tracks lie a few millimetres under the other pitches, so a field mapped on an infield, or a stadium's whole oval, shows on top. The 30 fields the data gives (12 soccer pitches, 8 tennis parks, 9 basketball courts, a track) are laid out by `fieldLines` in `paint.ts` from a centre, a direction and a size, and drawn as lane lines are: 10–12 cm marks that the vertex shader widens to a pixel, with the fine paint at the end of the cell's mesh, so they cost a zoomed-out frame nothing. A field stays whole in the cell of its centre, draped like the rest. Arcs take as many chords as keep each within 20 cm of its curve. All of them are 3.0k triangles and 9.1k vertices.
- **Railways ride along for free.** A track is stored as a centre line and drawn as two flat ribbons in its cell's mesh: a ballast bed, and a sleeper band whose `facade` attribute holds the distance along the track (as a negative code past `SLEEPER_CODE`), so the facade shader draws the sleepers (and fades them to a tint when they get smaller than a pixel). Both rails and the catenary masts are ink lines at the start of the cell's outlines. A track cut at a cell border carries how far along the whole track its piece starts (`from`, the `Track` type in `rails.ts`), so the sleepers and the masts, every 60 m, run on across the border. Zoomed out they'd merge into a dark hatch, so the viewer skips them with the outlines' draw range: masts show once a screen pixel is under 0.9 m, rails under 0.5 m (from about zoom 0.8 and 1.4 in an 800 px tall window on a standard screen, half that on a 2× one). Bridge parapets and platforms are a few quads more; platforms are cut at cell borders like the ground, so they never stretch a cell's culling box. Railway land is one more flat ground layer, the lowest, so grass and copses mapped on the embankments show, and Leuven's wave roof is four vaulted bays of 16 triangles, each in the cell of its centre. The whole railway costs about 2.6k triangles and no draw call (`rails.ts`).
- **Map controls of our own.** Panning and zooming the map is `map-controls.ts`: a drag keeps the ground under the finger, two fingers pinch about their middle and drag, the wheel zooms about the cursor (a notch of 100 px by 5%, lines and pages counted as pixels). While `pinned`, a pinch or the wheel zooms about the avatar instead, and only a drag past the 8 px tap slop lets go of it; under that slop the map holds still, so a tap never wobbles it. The arrow keys do nothing. three's `MapControls` did this with rotation turned off, for 4.4 KB of the first load where these take about 1 KB.
- **Render on demand.** Nothing renders unless the camera or the view changes, so an idle map uses no GPU. The one exception is asked for: with the day passing (P), a frame loop of its own advances the clock and draws every frame, and stops with it.
- **Traffic is eight instanced meshes and their ink.** The ways come baked from OSM (`src/data/traffic.ts`): roads, cycle tracks, pedestrian streets and paths, split on their shared nodes, cut where they would run through a building, each with both its sides measured (how far the carriageway reaches before the GRB's kerb, and how far the open ground goes on before a facade; docs/DATA.md). The viewer joins them into a graph of directed edges (cars, bikes and feet each allowed their own: nobody walks a main road without a pavement) and the traffic chunk (`vehicles.ts` and `traffic.ts`, loaded once the first frame is drawn) drives a fleet of up to 220 cars, 14 buses, 300 bikes and 300 people round the view, as many of each as the ways its class may use within the circle it spawns in hold at `PER_KM` (about a car every 270 m of street and a bike every 125 m of way, a quiet hour in Leuven: a thin network holds few, so Abdij van Park gets its floor of 30 cars where the centre gets 70), spawning in the ring between the view and the recycling radius, never in sight (a spot not found this step is found another; a fill after a jump lands in the middle of longer edges), and for cars only on the core of their network (`coreEdges`: what stays connected when any one road goes, less appendages under 1.5 km such as an abbey's lanes or a stub to the map's edge, and never a street for those who live there): each picks a destination among the edges near the focus (most cars one beyond the view, `THROUGH_GOALS`, so they cross it and leave rather than circle in it) and follows the cheapest route there for its class (a car pays five times to cut through a destination-only street and two and a half through a living street, which it drives at 20 km/h) (`GraphRouter` in `src/data/graph-route.ts`, an A* over any graph on a `MinHeap`, one router per class with its own costs so cars keep to through roads, bikes to cycle tracks and quiet streets, people to paths and pedestrian streets; four searches a frame at most, random turns until one comes), never one that starts by turning back at the junction ahead (the twin of its own edge is barred from the search, and edges that lead nowhere but back are out of the spawn and goal pools), keeps its distance from the one ahead in its lane (a trimmed intelligent-driver model: the vehicles sorted by edge and queue each step, so the leader is the next in line; on a street too narrow for a car to pass a bike the two share one queue), slows for the corner chosen next and rounds it on a quadratic curve from the corner's start to its end (`place`), swings its drawn heading after the path at a rate its kind can (`turnTowards`), signals the turn thirty metres out and lights its brakes when it slows, and takes a junction only when nobody else of its class holds it and there is room beyond (a claim per node and class, let go past the corner), where a junction is a node at which three or more of the class's ways meet (a path joining a street, or a way merely split, is none), and a vehicle that has stood at one for `PATIENCE` (6 s) crosses anyway: without both, chains of claims round a block locked the whole centre up within minutes (`scripts/traffic-flow.ts` measures it). Lanes come from the measured sides (`laneFor`): cars in the carriageway, bikes by its kerb, people on the pavement between kerb and facade, spread across it, or on the left pavement when the right has none; where no kerb was cut the ground is shared. Each kind is two `InstancedMesh` pairs with inverted ink hulls sharing their matrices: a far shape (a hatchback about 140 triangles, a bus 140, and the walker's figure squared off without its details or outline, about 290 on foot and 350 on the city bike) for the fleet, and a near shape the nearest `NEAR` of each kind move to each frame while the view's radius is within `NEAR_VIEW` (420 m) and they are within `NEAR_RANGE` (90 m) of its target: the walker's figure (`figure.ts`, about 870 triangles with its hull) on foot, riding the rideable bike (`bike-rig.ts`, about 1,600 together), a car or a bus with its detail (arches, pillars, mirrors, bumpers, grille, seams, window pillars, doors). Sixteen draw calls and up to 260k triangles with the whole fleet out zoomed out, about 150k more close in, nothing culled, nothing allocated per frame. The far figure was checked against the walker up close by drawing every figure in its far shape (`NEAR_RANGE` set to 0 for the check): [docs/figure-far-beside-walker.jpg](figure-far-beside-walker.jpg) shows a cyclist in it beside the walker, the same figure at the same size without its bevels and outline. The figure's bones swing in the vertex shader (`FIGURE_GLSL`): the walker's gait and the rider's pedalling fitted to three harmonics a joint by `scripts/gait-fit.ts`, each chain turning about its own joint then its parents' as the skeleton does, the bike's wheels and crank turning with the stride; a far shape's box limbs swing about the joint folded into their part code. Per instance, `paint` (the shirt), `trim` (shorts, or the bike's frame), `skin` and `hair` colour the figure's faces times their own shade, and `state` (the stride, the brakes, the indicator, a blink offset) drives the limbs and the lamps. Each vehicle stands on the terrain sampled under its front and its back, pitched between them (`settle`), on a square's higher setts where its edge crosses one, so wheels and feet neither sink nor float on Leuven's slopes. Headlamps, tail lamps, a bike's lamp and reflector glow with the night uniform under dithered halos, and each car, bus and bike throws a beam on the street ahead at night, three strips that dither away to the sides and the far end. Vehicles take the toon shading and the static shadow map but cast none. While traffic runs (or the day passes) the viewer keeps a frame loop of its own and draws about 30 frames a second on the map (`LIVE_FPS`; the avatar's loop draws every frame anyway while it moves); it is the one thing that stops render-on-demand, and C stops it.
- **Day and night cost nothing but a redraw of the shadows.** The light at an hour (\`daylight.ts\`, pure, tested) sets the sun's and the hemisphere light's colours and intensities, the fog, the page's sky tokens through \`onDaylight\`, and one \`uNight\` uniform the facade shader reads: how many windows and shop fronts are lit, as emissive light the toon shading leaves alone. The shadow map's sun moves in quarter-hour steps (\`sunStep\`), each a refit and a redraw of the static shadow map, so a passing day redraws it every two seconds rather than every frame. The lamps' light is a mesh per cell built with the furniture (\`addLampGlow\`: a lit lantern and a 12-sided pool on the street, 22 triangles a lamp), drawn by an unlit material that discards fragments in the ordered dither by how far on the lamps are (\`createGlowMaterial\`): no blending, no sorting, one draw call a cell at night at every zoom, nothing by day.
- **Hard shadows with a soft rim, four lookups.** Shadows use three's PCF map, with its five-tap disc that turns per pixel swapped for four hardware-filtered taps in a fixed square about a texel across (`shadow-filter.ts`): a shadow's edge becomes a ramp of about three texels instead of a stair-step, still with no penumbra, and nothing shimmers as the view moves. Each lookup starts one and a half shadow texels off its surface on the sun's side, whichever way the face is turned (three would push a face turned from the sun into itself, and the map holds back faces), and only 5 cm nearer the sun. The offset is set whenever the square is fitted (`shadowBias` in `shadow-fit.ts`), so it is 19 cm from the street and 38 cm at zoom 4: benches and statues keep their shadows, and walls in grazing light don't break into stripes. Before, a fixed 0.6 m offset and a 1.4 m depth bias lifted every lookup above anything under about 1.4 m.
- **Static shadows, fitted to the view.** The city doesn't move, so the shadow map renders only on load and when the view leaves the square it covers (or zooms in far enough to deserve sharper shadows). The square is fitted to what's on screen, with 30% room to pan, a size stepped in 19% increments and a centre snapped to whole texels, so shadow edges don't swim when it moves (`shadow-fit.ts`). Its texels stay as small as the old whole-map shadows were at zoom 1 and get sharper zoomed in: the map redraws the square once it is 2× coarser than the view needs, with min(60 m, a quarter of the view's height) of room round it, 21 cm a texel at zoom 4. On a frame that redraws it, the casting cells show a shadow-only mesh on the shadow camera's layer, so the shadow pass draws only them and the view only what's in view. That mesh shares its cell's buffers, index included, and its draw range starts past the flat ground and the tree trunks, which each cell writes first: the ground can't shade anything, and from above a trunk's shadow hides in its crown's. Close to the street, where it doesn't, the trunks cast too. Only close to the street (`streetShadows`, under 60 m) do trunks and furniture cast: further out their shadows would be thinner than a texel.
- **No hidden walls, no idle vertices.** A wall between two touching buildings is drawn only above the lower one (`partyWalls`). The bake drops footprint vertices within 0.25 m (0.5 m on the fringe) of the line through their neighbours; hero footprints keep theirs, as landmark models are fitted to them. Trees are a 4-sided open trunk and a 20-face crown, 28 triangles.
- **One rendering path.** The canvas renders at device resolution, capped at 2×. Only standard-density screens (below 1.5×) get multisampling; dense screens are smooth without it.

## One camera, one avatar

There are no modes. The map's orthographic camera at the true isometric pitch is the only camera: it pans, turns in quarters and zooms from the whole city (zoom 0.5) down to a street about 14 m tall (zoom 40), and one avatar is on Grote Markt from the first frame. Everything worth drawing is a threshold on one number, the view's height where it looks (`span`, `VIEW_HEIGHT / zoom`), in one tested table (`detailAt` in `lod.ts`):

| span (m) | drawn                                                                                  |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| > 400    | the cells in view: the city only; the avatar is the app's HTML arrow, not in the scene |
| 120–400  | nothing more (`marker` in `lod.ts` draws nothing since the diamond went)               |
| ≤ 280    | + street furniture, parked bikes, shop fronts' awnings and signs                       |
| ≤ 120    | + the avatar's figure, and the street plate naming where it stands                     |
| ≤ 60     | + trunks and furniture cast shadows, rooms near it built ahead                         |

- **The far end of the street fades, not the camera's mode.** Fog is a function of the span (`fogRange`): none from 120 m up, and close in a little haze over the top of the screen, the far end of the street. It is always in the scene, so zooming never recompiles a shader.
- **The camera is pinned, never mounted.** While `MapControls.pinned`, the view's centre glides after the avatar on a 0.18 s half-life (`follow.ts`), a third of a second of travel ahead of it, so the street ahead shows. A drag unpins it and the view is the person's until Space (or Esc), W A S D, R or the edge arrow pins it again; a place from the menu (`lookAt`) unpins it too and glides there instead, down to zoom 8. The camera only translates: its pitch is the map's and its yaw stays in quarters. W A S D walk by the screen, up away from the camera, whichever way the map is turned.
- **A tap goes to what it touched.** The avatar layer draws the one pixel under the tap again with three's depth material, into a 1×1 target through a view offset (`pickAt`; `unpackDepth` in `pick.ts` reads its four bytes back), and unprojects it: the exact roof, wall, crown, bench, floor or figure the person sees there, in well under a millisecond, with no shadow pass. What that point means is `aimFor` in `walkable.ts`: ground stays where it is, a building is left by half-metre steps toward the camera from a wall facing it, or toward the avatar from a roof, and a point on the avatar stops it. It used to march the view ray through the 1 m building grid, which can't see trees, furniture or the shape of a roof: a tap on a crown landed up to 14 m behind the tree, and one on the avatar about 3 m behind it.
- **A tap sends the avatar, within reach.** Within 250 m of it, it walks (or rides) there along a route (`route.ts`). Further, or where there is no ground, a greyed marker drops on the spot and shakes its head (`marker.ts`, one unlit draw call while it plays) and `onBlocked` says why. Nothing jumps the avatar but a building's door (Rooms, below). One tap test in the viewer beside the map controls' pan, pinch and wheel: nothing else listens to the pointer.
- **Its own chunk, after the first frame.** The avatar's code (`avatar-layer.ts`: the figure, the walk grid and routes, the refusal marker, the follow, and the rooms' code with it) is a chunk the viewer imports once the first frame is drawn, as the furniture and the traffic are. At the default view the avatar isn't drawn, so the first frame misses nothing.
- **Animate only while something moves.** The avatar's loop runs while it moves, turns, idles (seven seconds, and only while its figure shows), or the camera glides after it or to a place; then it stops, and the map is back to rendering on demand. Nothing is allocated per frame: the collision queries and a floor's stairs reuse their scratch.
- **Walkability is built on demand.** `WalkGrid` (`src/data/walkable.ts`) rasterises the ground at 1 m in 64 m tiles the first time something asks about them: open, minus water and railway, plus roads, squares, paths and bridge decks, minus buildings, tanks and parapets, then trunks and street furniture (`src/scene/solids.ts`, built from the same positions and sizes the scene draws: about 11,600 posts and 2,700 footprints, bucketed by tile once, 20 ms on the first walk). A trunk, bench, rack, statue or café table closes the cells whose centres it covers, at no height, so routes go round it and the camera and clicks pass over it; a post thinner than 25 cm (a lamp) closes none, so a narrow pavement with a lamp on it keeps its route, and the walker steps round the post itself. The bikes left about the city come and go, so they aren't in the grid: a route closes the cells they cover for its own search (`Router.route`'s `avoid` circles, from `loose` in `collide.ts`). A click costs one A* search (`route.ts`) inside a window around both ends, a few milliseconds, then string-pulling into a handful of straight legs.
- **Two draw calls for the figure.** It's a few blocks on a fourteen-bone `SkinnedMesh`, at most 900 triangles with their outlines (a test holds it there; 877 now, 459 before the clothes, hands and face got their detail, 746 with the old coat, scarf and backpack). Only parts that make the silhouette get an ink hull; the details inside it (collar, placket, buttons, pocket, belt, seams, the face) are flat quads a few millimetres proud, a couple of triangles each. The ink outline is an inverted hull in the same geometry, and the blob shadow is dithered by the shader instead of blended, so figure, outline and shadow are one call. Its x-ray twin, a flat coral silhouette at 45% opacity drawn only where a building hides it, is the second; it is one small mesh, so blending it costs next to nothing. The route hint is one more while a walk is under way; dots behind the walker drop off by moving its draw range.
- **Feet on the ground as drawn.** The avatar, its bike and the bikes left about the city stand on the terrain plus the layer drawn there (a pavement 0.15 m over the terrain, a square 0.2 m), as the parked bikes do: the avatar host's `groundAt` is the viewer's `drawnAt`, which reads `drawnGround` from the furniture chunk (the pavement's height until that has loaded). Feet and tyres stand 4 cm over it (`FEET_Y` in `avatar-layer.ts`), so their ink never dips into the street, and a kerb's step up or down eases over a 40 ms half-life (`KERB_EASE`) rather than in one frame.
- **The figure doesn't cast into the static shadow map**, but it receives it, so it darkens in the shade of the houses it passes.

### Hidden, and found

At 35° a house across a 6 m street hides the avatar much of the time, and the view stays there at every zoom: a steeper camera barely clears Leuven's 6–8 m streets while flattening the facades (at 65° a 7 m street is still 64% hidden, and a facade keeps 42% of its height). The see-through opening that used to dither away whatever stood in front (`occlusion.ts`) is gone, and with it the city material's and the plates' two uniforms.

- **Behind a house, a silhouette.** The figure's x-ray twin (`createXrayMaterial` in `character-material.ts`) is a flat fill in its shirt's coral at 45% opacity, drawn only where something nearer filled the depth buffer, so an open street shows nothing and the houses never change. The rider's twin (`bikes.ts`) is still the old blue checkerboard for now.
- **Far out or off screen, an arrow.** The viewer reports where the avatar's feet land on the canvas and whether its figure is drawn (`onAvatarAt`, only when either moves by a pixel). The app turns that into one HTML arrow (`pointerFor` in `src/app/pointer.ts`, `.avatar-arrow` in `app.css`): over the avatar's head, bobbing, while the view is over 120 m tall; at the edge of the screen, inside insets of 76, 32, 88 and 32 px (top, right, bottom, left) that keep it clear of the HUD, on a paper disc pointing the way, while the avatar is off screen, where a tap pins the camera again. Placed by `translate`, so a pan moves it without a layout: 0.4 KB of the app shell and nothing in the scene.

### How walking should feel

Movement follows the rules third-person games have settled on, so walking reads as smooth on any screen. Constants live next to the code (`walker.ts`, `avatar.ts`, `follow.ts`):

- **Nothing eases by a fixed share per frame.** `x += (goal - x) * k` eases faster on a 120 Hz screen than at 60. Every easing is an exponential decay or a critically damped spring with a half-life (`motion.ts`), exact for any frame time ([Holmér, "Lerp smoothing is broken"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LSNQuFEDOyQ); [Holden, "Spring-It-On"](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)).
- **Fixed-step simulation, drawn in between.** The walker steps at 120 Hz whatever the display does, and each frame draws it blended between its last two steps ([Fiedler, "Fix Your Timestep!"](https://gafferongames.com/post/fix_your_timestep/)). The same keys walk the same path at 30, 60 or 144 fps, and a frame never shows a step taken early or late.
- **Speed and heading are springs, not ramps.** Speed and heading carry their own rate of change, so setting off, stopping and turning curve in and out instead of snapping. The largest frame-to-frame jump in turn rate fell from about 20 rad/s to 3 to 7. Turning is quick standing still (0.07 s half-life) and wider at a run (0.14 s), and a sharp turn slows the walk first. At the end of a route the walker brakes and coasts the last few centimetres instead of stopping dead. Let go, it stops within a third of a second (a 60 ms half-life, standing below 0.1 m/s), in about a metre at a run.
- **Collide and slide, against walls as drawn.** The body is a circle of 0.45 m radius, the character controller's "collide and slide" ([Fauerby, "Improved Collision detection and Response"](https://www.peroxide.dk/papers/collision/collision.pdf)): a step that would run into a wall loses the part that goes into it and slides along instead, at the share of its pace that's left (the cosine of the angle), and whatever it still overlaps pushes it back out at up to 2 m/s, never in a jump. Walking straight at a wall stops at once rather than creeping; a second wall met while sliding (a corner) stops it too, so it doesn't jitter between them. Walls are the footprints', tanks' and parapets' outlines, not the 1 m walk cells (`WalkGrid.wallOverlap`, filed by tile like the rest of the grid): on cells, a facade at 8° to the grid wobbled the body by up to a metre from step to step, and the old axis probes stopped dead at the steps. Along any facade the gap now holds within a few centimetres, a convex corner is a point the body rounds, and a lane a metre wide between two houses lets it through, both walls pushing until it's centred. The edge of the water and the tracks is no wall: the feet (0.15 m) keep off it, through a bilinear blend of the closed cells, so the quay is a nearly straight line too. A step costs three overlap checks of a few dozen segment distances: about 4 µs running along Krakenstraat's facades, under 10 µs a frame. The camera no longer looks anything up at all; it used to make fifty to a few hundred grid and tree lookups a frame.
- **A brisk pace.** Walking is 5.4 m/s and running 9.2 m/s, a cartoon figure a third larger than life. A bike is 11.5 m/s cruising and 17.5 flat out (below).
- **Feet stay where they land.** The pose is placed feet first (`poseAt` in `character.ts`): the gait cycle puts each ankle where it should be, and the legs reach it by two-bone IK. A foot that is down slides back under the hips exactly as fast as the walker moves on, so in the world it stays put: flat, then rolling over the ball of the foot as the heel lifts. Its swing is a Hermite curve that leaves and lands at most of that speed, so it neither scuffs off nor skids in. The hips ride as high as both legs reach (a soft minimum), which bobs a walk; a run, down under a third of each cycle, sinks onto each step and springs into half a step with both feet in the air. A stride is what the leg can sweep: 2.6 m walking (249 steps a minute) and 6.4 m running (268). The faster pace mostly lengthens the stride rather than quickening the steps (they were 2.1 and 4.8 m, 229 and 265 a minute), so the legs read as brisk rather than frantic: the walk centres its steps a little ahead of the hips on slightly bent knees, so the longer reach costs the bob nothing, and the run's feet are down a fifth of each cycle, not a quarter, for a longer flight. With the old swinging legs the planted foot slid about 1.2 m a step walking and 2.2 m running; now under a millimetre, which a test holds below 0.1 m against the real walker. Below 1.5 m/s the steps shrink to standing, the walker's acceleration leans the body and moves the hips ahead of the feet or behind them, and turning on the spot lifts the feet in turn, a step for every 45° of heading. The lean follows the walker's `surge`, its acceleration eased over 0.1 s: letting go, the speed spring's acceleration leaps in one step, which leaned the body back 0.15 rad from one frame to the next, and now takes a few. A foot lets go of the hips gradually after it lifts off (half its swing walking, a tenth running) and takes hold again before it lands, and the reach never pulls the hips more than 12 cm below where they ride, so they rise and sink rather than jump as feet come and go: under 4 cm a step at a steady walk or run, where it was 9.
- **More than legs.** The hips dip 0.05 rad on the side of the swinging foot and turn against the shoulders by half the torso's twist (0.2 rad walking, 0.12 running); the legs undo that turn, as they undo the roll, so the gait's IK is unchanged but for each hip joint moving a centimetre. The arms swing 0.55 rad walking and 0.8 running, and the one swinging forward bends at the elbow. Stopping, the body rocks on once and the head nods after it, gone in half a second. Idling (`IDLE_SECONDS`, 7 s, three more than before, for the weight shift) it breathes three times, glances 0.55 rad aside, and drops its hips 0.11 rad onto one leg and back, the other knee bending by IK and the shoulders levelling against it, all faded in and out so the last frame is the rest pose. Turning on the spot, the head leads the turn by up to 0.4 rad.
- **Landing (`settle`).** `poseAt`'s last argument, from 0 as the feet touch the ground to 1 (the default) settled, lands the figure: `landing(settle)` is a quick dip that bottoms out a sixth of the way in and rebounds a little, gone by the end; it lowers the hips 14 cm at its deepest (the knees bend by IK, the feet stay planted), leans the body over them, lifts the head and brings the arms forward. Nothing runs it now: nothing drops the avatar anywhere, and going through a door sets it down standing, with a thud.

### Bikes

A bike is the avatar at a bike's paces (`ON_BIKE` in `avatar.ts`): faster walking with a bell. Its chunk (`bikes.ts`, 3.1 KB, with `audio/riding.ts`) loads when the avatar comes within 25 m of a parked bike, or on F.

- **Walking's handling, a bike's paces.** The same walker step with the same setting-off, stopping and turning half-lives (`ON_BIKE = { ...ON_FOOT, walk: 11.5, run: 17.5, body: 0.5, stride: 5 }`): only its look, its speed and its pedalling differ, and a tap within 250 m rides there as it walks there. It used to have momentum of its own (a 0.9 s half-life setting off, 0.45 s coasting, turns widening with speed).
- **The traffic's own cyclist.** The rider is one instance of the traffic's near cyclist shape and its ink hull (`NEAR_SHAPES.bike`, `vehicleMaterials` in `vehicles.ts`), pedalling in the vertex shader at the traffic's stride, leaning 0.45 of a real bike's lean into turns (at most 16°), with an x-ray twin (for now still the blue checkerboard the walker's was): three draw calls. The rider's pose, the kinematic bicycle and the scripted mounts went with the chase camera.
- **Taking a bike from its rack draws the rack copy as nothing**: its index range in the cell's furniture is zeroed (degenerate triangles) and only that range uploaded, with what was there kept to put back; the shadow map redraws once. For this the viewer keeps the index arrays of the furniture meshes that hold bikes; a lost GPU context builds those again with every rack full, so the bikes taken are zeroed again in the new ones.
- **Getting on and off is a 0.6 s glide** onto the bike or a metre out to its left; F within 30 m walks there first. A bike left standing leans on its kickstand where you got off, on the ground under each wheel and pitched between them as a parked bike is, its blob shadow level on the ground whatever the lean (leaned with it, half the shadow sank into the street and flickered); up to four are baked into one mesh, and the oldest goes back to its rack.
- **Sounds are events:** a freewheel pawl as the wheel passes one (six a turn), the bell, the kickstand.

### Rooms

Sint-Pieterskerk has an inside (`public/data/rooms.json`, `src/data/rooms.ts`), in the same world space as the street: no second scene, no loading screen. Its door is the one thing that puts the avatar anywhere at once.

- **Opening a landmark costs no rebuild.** Landmarks are merged into their cell's mesh, so the cell notes each one's index range in its mesh and outlines (`LandmarkRange` in `cells.ts`) and keeps those two index arrays. Going in zeroes the range (as a taken bike's), uploads only it and redraws the shadow map once; going out puts it back. The building's walls, cut down to the room's ceiling (`addBuilding`'s `cut`) with no roof, tower or parts, are drawn in the room's ground-floor mesh instead. Rebuilding the cell with an `open` set, as the plan had it, would cost a cell's build on the way in; this costs a few kilobytes of kept indices for the six cells with landmarks.
- **A floor is a mesh and its ink**, two draw calls, each with its own bounds (`room-mesh.ts`): the slab (bluestone on the ground floor, boards above, its edges round the stairwell), walls 30 cm thick between their gaps with every face turned out of the wall, so the face looking into the room is the one the camera sees, the furnishings and the stair's steps; on the ground floor also the building's cut walls, lined inside, and its whole footprint floored under the slab. The church's ground floor is about 2,700 triangles with its cut walls, the gallery about 260. Floors above the avatar are hidden, so no ceiling is ever seen; the floors below show. Inside: 4 calls for the floors, the church's own range drawn as nothing.
- **Each floor is walked on its own grid** (`RoomGrid`, the street's raster at half a metre, `cell-grid.ts`): the slab less its holes and furnishings, plus its stairs, met at its walls as drawn. A stair is a corridor between rails, shut at its top end on the floor below; halfway up, the avatar counts as on the floor above, and its feet follow the ramp, so the switch shows no seam (`standing` in `rooms.ts`, tested up and down).
- **In by Enter, out either way.** Within 5 m of the building's footprint, from any side, on foot, Enter (or the Go in pill) puts the avatar 3 m in from the door, on the nearest open spot of the room's grid; inside, it puts it 2 m in front of the door on the street's. The door is open only in the room's grid: the street's has no doorway any more (`WalkGrid.openDoor` is gone), so walking in through it isn't possible, while walking out through the gap still leaves, once 0.3 m past the footprint's edge at the door, so brushing the edge doesn't flicker. The canvas's `data-door` names the room beside or around the avatar. The floor eases from the street's height to the room's over the first 2 m in. The room's floor stands over the highest ground drawn under it, sampled every 3 m.
- **Inside, the camera keeps to zoom 8–20.** A room's floors and the church's cut walls are built while the page is idle when the avatar is within 60 m of its door and the view close to the street; one room is held built ahead at a time, besides the one shown. `rooms.json` is fetched near a landmark (1.2 KB); a failed fetch is said in the hint, the door stays shut, and it is tried again on a later approach.

### What other apps do, and what we took

- **Occlusion: let the house stand, show the player through it.** Third-person games either pull the camera in for whatever blocks the line of sight (Unreal's spring arm, Cinemachine's [Deoccluder](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineDeoccluder.html)) or fade what stands in front, usually by dithering rather than blending ([Opsive's object fader](https://opsive.com/support/documentation/ultimate-character-controller/camera/object-fader/)). Top-down games and maps (Diablo IV, The Sims, Google Maps and others) mostly keep their angle and let a building hide the player, drawing a silhouette of them behind it. A fixed isometric camera has nowhere to move to, and tilting it steeper barely clears Leuven's narrow streets (above), so we keep 35.26° and Diablo IV's flat silhouette; the dithered opening we had went.
- **Cutaways, as the Sims and Two Point games do.** A building you enter loses its roof and its walls are cut down, floors above hidden, so a room is read from above without a ceiling ever showing.
- **Collision response: collide and slide.** Character controllers sweep a capsule and slide along what they hit by taking out the part of the move that goes into it ([Fauerby](https://www.peroxide.dk/papers/collision/collision.pdf)); a capsule's round end rounds corners, and a second contact in the same step is a crease where it stops. A grid of 1 m cells is too coarse for the walls themselves (a slanted facade is a staircase), so the body meets the footprints as drawn and the grid only for closed ground.
- **Turning and stopping.** Games turn on the spot at a walk and in an arc at a run, with a heavier turn rate the faster you go, and stop within a few frames when the stick is let go ([Nesky, "50 Game Camera Mistakes"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-50-common-game-camera-mistakes----and-how-to-fix-them) covers the camera side). Our springs already did the turn; stopping got crisper.
- **Holding a turn button or key.** Repeating controls wait, then repeat: Chromium's own repeating button waits 500 ms ([`repeating_button.js`](https://crrev.com/f968f610359dabba8a722e45b10aa28d5bba5a04/ui/webui/resources/js/cr/ui/repeating_button.js)), its views `RepeatController` 250 ms and then every 50 ms ([source](https://mojo.googlesource.com/mojo/+/02b32f11dbffc023b3d5f849fb09e79dab51349d/ui/views/repeat_controller.cc)). Google Earth's compass arrows move the view for as long as they're held ([help](https://support.google.com/earth/answer/148186)). MapLibre's NavigationControl has no turn buttons, and its keys turn 15° per keydown, auto-repeats included, each a fresh 300 ms ease ([`keyboard.ts`](https://github.com/maplibre/maplibre-gl-js/blob/main/src/ui/handler/keyboard.ts)), which pulses when held. Repeated quarter turns would pulse the same way, and a free turn would stop between the views the isometric map is drawn for. So a press steps at once, as a tap always has; held past 300 ms (the step has landed), the map turns on at a steady quarter per half second, through the quarter views without stopping, and let go it brakes onto the next quarter view ahead, never back (`spin.ts`). Keys go by keydown and keyup, not auto-repeat.
- **Small colliders, met as drawn.** A game gives a trunk a collider; so do we, for the 9,100 trunks (capped at a 35 cm radius, so they aren't a slalom) and the 4,800 props: each is a circle or a box in the same collide and slide as the walls, about 0.25 µs more a step. On a route the walker follows the grid's cells, which close where a solid covers their centre.

## Growing the city

The research plan (REPORT.md §2) has three detail tiers, and each building already carries one:

- **hero:** hand-fitted landmarks (Stadhuis, Sint-Pieterskerk, Universiteitsbibliotheek, Station Leuven, Arenbergkasteel, the Abdij van Park church) and the most stepped gables. A landmark's footprint is listed in `LANDMARK_FOOTPRINTS` (`area.ts`), so the bake keeps it whole and in the hero tier.
- **core:** procedural Flemish houses with gables.
- **fringe:** plain blocks.

The map already covers the whole R23 ring and Heverlee. Growing it further:

- Zoomed out is the heaviest view. If it nears the budget, add a coarser level of detail for cells far from the centre of the screen, or raise the minimum zoom, before raising a budget.
- Fringe buildings could skip outlines, or the shadow pass, if they start to dominate.

Run the smoke test after each step and watch the numbers.

WebGPU and TSL post-processing (outlines, palette quantisation, dithering) are the next step for the look. Move to `WebGPURenderer` only when an effect needs it, and keep the smoke budgets green.
